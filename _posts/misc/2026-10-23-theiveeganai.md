## Translation

**Divine Dog / Become a Divine Person ** - *Kovirai (Pen name translated from Rajesh)*

The divine dog—Bhairava; the buffalo—Yama; the Sheep—Agni; the elephant—Indra; the horse—Surya;

The serpent—Shiva; the crow—Shani. Why are gods associated with animals?

The Sun's steed, the bull, the rooster, the mouse, the tiger, the fish, the tortoise, and the boar—

The lion that Hari became, and many other lives: those diminished by slaying them all with an axe, are they human?

Monkey, bear, vulture, squirrel, peacock, and the mother among cattle;

Buffalo and goat that give milk—is it the Kali age that even one that's a Buddha eats them?

Through crooked conduct, Dharma declines; of own accord, one falls into the net that's cast.

Tormenting animals to eat and grow one's flesh—is this to be born again wearing fetters?

Those who maintain tender compassion toward every life are the righteous.

Hostility and anger fall away from any who nears their presence.

Valluvar, Patanjali, and many other sages—

Have given mighty testimony to non-harming.

Milk and clear honey, meat and eggs;

Leather and silk, coral and pearls—all renounced;

Without needlessly killing animals, become a vegan—

Shine as a wondrous light; become divine!

## Commentary

Structure

Poetic Ornamentaion

Metaphors and Core Messages

The core message of the poem is the case for veganism: it suggests that one's awe of divinity, one's humanity, one's spiritual enlightenemnt, one's desire for salvation, and the scriptural authority of Valluvar and Patanjali all point to adopting veganism, characterizes it and elevates it to being a divine position. 

It drives home the point by placing on an equal footing the dog, the horse, the boar, the cow, the buffalo, the goat, the rooster, the fish, and other creatures: the implication is that they are all equal and sacred, and humans ought not to exploit, eat, and/or kill them. It also argues that the maternal sacredness acoorded to an animal that gives milk ought to be extended to the others -- a messaage of universal benevolence toward living beings and non-harming that Valluvar and Patanjali spoke of.
The implicit corrollary is that taboos on eating an animal should be extended to the others as well. The poem predominatly invokes the imagery of Indic cultural milieu and spiritual traditions, which dovetails nicely with the invocation of the scriptural authority of Valluvar and Patanjali, who are part of those Indic traditions, and reading anything further into the poetic ornamentaion will be counter-productive.

The poem moves from the association of animals with divinity to the questioning of their killing and consumption, then to the bondage created by harmful conduct, the authority of sages, and finally the positive practice of veganism.

Mythological allusion. The opening catalogue invokes the six major devotional traditions—Saura, Shaiva, Kaumara, Ganapatya, Shakta, and Vaishnava—while anticipating the Dashavatara. The Sun's steeds, Hari as Narasimha, the axe of Parashurama, and kurukuvar, suggestive of Vamana, embed the avatar references in the animal imagery. The monkeys, bears, vultures, and squirrels evoke the Ramayana; the peacock and cow evoke Krishna. Mythology is not merely decorative: animals associated with the divine become the very lives whose treatment is questioned.

Equality beyond selective taboos. The juxtaposition of dog, horse, pig, cow, and buffalo challenges the selective protection of particular species. The deliberate equation of the buffalo with the cow as a mother extends the ethical claim beyond the cow's special religious status. The poem leaves its critique of religious and cultural distinctions implicit: the reader must confront why one animal is protected while another is killed.

The many senses of vilangu. The poem's semantic chain encompasses animality, crooked or non-upright conduct, a net or snare, and fetters. In vilanganiyum pirappu, birth is figured as wearing the fetters of embodied existence, recalling the Gita's image of the embodied self changing garments (vāsāṃsi jīrṇāni, 2.22). The suggestion of animal rebirth remains a possible resonance, but the deeper point is bondage through embodiment.

From non-harming to exemplarity. The allusion to Valluvar paraphrases the virtue of tender compassion toward all life. The Patanjali reference evokes Yoga Sutra 2.35: ahiṃsā-pratiṣṭhāyāṃ tat-sannidhau vaira-tyāgaḥ—when one is firmly established in non-harming, hostility is relinquished in that person's presence. Non-harming becomes a transformative presence, not merely a rule.

Sound as meaning. The poem's free-form structure nevertheless employs classical poetic resources. Śabdālaṅkāra binds the opening தெய்வீகநாய் to the closing தெய்வீகனாய், transforming the divine dog into the person who becomes divine. The interplay of vilangu/vilakku and viḷangu/viḷakku, the four senses of vilangu, and the rhyme between putthelir and putthaneyanium interweave sound, semantic ambiguity, and allusion. The Sangam word putthelir also anticipates the later invocation of Valluvar.

Finally, theiyā veganai—the vegan who does not kill—leads into theiveeganai, becoming divine. The closing movement from abstention to shining light and divinity completes the poem's argument: non-harming is not merely a prohibition; it is a way of becoming.
