## Comments and Research Notes

### Verse 1

I propose Arthashastra 7.12.31-32 as the primary parallel for this kural. Valluvar's அழிவதூஉம் ஆவதூஉம் finds its direct counterpart in Kautilya's क्षयः वृद्धिः, while the opening अल्पागमातिव्ययता identifies the financial condition that produces decline: inadequate revenue combined with excessive expenditure; the reverse produces growth. This causal formulation illuminates வழிபயக்கும் ஊதியம்: the net return of an undertaking, determined by आय and व्यय, is what may lead either to அழிவு or ஆக்கம். The concluding कर्मसु connects directly with Valluvar's செயல், while ज्ञेयम् supplies the deliberative injunction expressed by சூழ்ந்து: the financial position of the undertaking must be assessed before proceeding. Kautilya specifically requires equal attention to revenue and expenditure, rather than considering either in isolation.

The accounting concept underlying this assessment is further clarified by Arthashastra 2.6.27: संजातादायव्ययविशुद्धा नीवी, प्राप्ता चानुवृत्ता च \|\|. Kautilya defines नीवी as the balance remaining after the reckoning of आय and व्यय, distinguishing between a newly received balance and one carried forward. Valluvar's ஊதியம் may be understood in relation to this difference between revenue and expenditure. This is a conceptual correspondence rather than an assertion of technical identity: the precise accounting treatment of items such as interest on debt, taxes, depreciation, and amortization may differ between the two systems.

The broader statecraft dimension of அழிவு, ஆக்கம், and ஊதியம் emerges from Kauṭilya's preceding formulation in Arthashastra 7.12.29-30: परकर्मोदयो नेतुः क्षयो वृद्धिर्विपर्यये \| तुल्ये कर्मपथे स्थानं ज्ञेयं स्वं विजिगीषुणा \|\| Here क्षय and वृद्धि describe the decline or advancement of the ruler arising from the outcome of an undertaking, rather than merely a commercial loss or gain. Read in this wider context, Valluvar's அழிவு and ஆக்கம், and the ஊதியம் that may lead to either, need not be confined to monetary consequences. They may encompass the broader consequences and returns of an action for the ruler and the state.

### Verse 6

I propose Arthashastra 7.5.21 and 7.5.26 as the primary parallels for this kural. The correspondence in 7.5.21 is particularly close: செய்தக்க வல்ல செயக்கெடும் finds its counterpart in अकार्याणां च करणैः (doing what ought not to be done), while செய்தக்க செய்யாமை யானுங் matches कार्याणां च प्रणाशनैः (frustrating what ought to be done). Even Valluvar's யானுங் finds an echo in Kautilya's च, reinforcing the pairing of the two forms of misconduct. The consequences captured by கெடும் are developed in 7.5.26: योगक्षेमवधेन (through the failure to secure the welfare and security of the subjects) leads to क्षयः, लोभः, and विराग्यम् (impoverishment, greed, and disaffection). 

The significance of this correspondence becomes clearer from the larger passage. Arthashastra 7.5.19–25 develops the principle through a series of paired forms of misgovernance. These include insulting the good and favoring the wicked; permitting unjustified violence; abandoning righteous customs and neglecting dharma; doing what ought not to be done and frustrating what ought to be done; withholding what is due and exacting what is not due; failing to punish the guilty and punishing the innocent; arresting those who should not be seized and releasing those who should be apprehended; undertaking unprofitable works and frustrating profitable ones; failing to protect the people from thieves and robbing them oneself; abandoning enterprise and condemning good works; harming leaders and dishonoring the worthy; provoking elders and acting with partiality and falsehood; failing to remedy wrongs; and neglecting work already undertaken. The passage thus elaborates Valluvar's compact pairing of wrongful action and omission across the full range of royal administration.

From Arthashastra 7.5.26 onward, Kauṭilya turns to the consequences of such misgovernance. Through the king's negligence, indolence, and failure to secure the welfare and security of his subjects, impoverishment, greed, and disaffection arise. The progression is particularly significant: the impoverished become greedy, the greedy become disaffected, and the disaffected may defect to the enemy or turn against their own ruler. The king must therefore prevent these conditions from arising or remedy them without delay. This gives a distinctly political dimension to Valluvar's கெடும்: the consequences of doing what ought not to be done, or neglecting what ought to be done, extend beyond the immediate undertaking to the deterioration of the kingdom and the ruler's own position.

Yoga Vasistha 6.126.54 provides a positive ethical and spiritual counterpart: कर्तव्यमाचरन्काममकर्तव्यमनाचरन् \| तिष्ठति प्राकृताचारो यः स आर्य इति स्मृतः \|\| (One who willingly performs what ought to be done and refrains from what ought not to be done, living according to proper conduct, is regarded as noble.) The pairing कर्तव्यमाचरन् and अकर्तव्यमनाचरन् directly echoes the two complementary obligations in Valluvar's formulation (செய்தக்க வல்ல and செய்தக்க செய்யாமை). The distinction is in emphasis: the kural warns of the consequences of violating these obligations, whereas the Yoga Vasistha presents their observance as the mark of noble conduct, particularly in the spiritual pursuit.

Mahabharata, Shanti Parva 12.35.2 (BORI) offers another corroboration, extending the consequences from worldly deterioration to moral accountability. Vyasa declares: अकुर्वन्विहितं कर्म प्रतिषिद्धानि चाचरन् \| प्रायश्चित्तीयते ह्येवं नरो मिथ्या च वर्तयन् \|\|. (A person becomes liable to expiation by failing to perform prescribed duties, engaging in prohibited acts, and conducting himself improperly.) The pairing of अकुर्वन् विहितं कर्म (failing to perform prescribed action) and प्रतिषिद्धानि चाचरन् (performing prohibited actions) closely corresponds to Valluvar's twofold formulation. The Mahabharata goes further by explicitly connecting such conduct with प्रायश्चित्त, the need for expiation.

Manusmriti 11.44 reinforces the same connection: अकुर्वन् विहितं कर्म निन्दितं च समाचरन् \| प्रसक्तश्चेन्द्रियार्थेषु प्रायश्चित्तीयते नरः \|\|. (Failing to perform prescribed duties, performing censured acts, and becoming attached to sense-objects make a person liable to expiation.) The correspondence with the Mahabharata is particularly close in the shared expressions अकुर्वन् विहितं कर्म and प्रायश्चित्तीयते. Manusmriti thus corroborates both the prescribed-action/prohibited-action pairing and its connection with expiation, while adding attachment to sense-objects as a further cause of transgression.

A concluding observation: The parallels reveal three complementary dimensions of Valluvar's compact formulation:

  - Statecraft: Kauṭilya demonstrates how wrongful action and neglect undermine the ruler, the subjects, and the kingdom.
  - Ethical ideal: The Yoga Vasistha presents the converse—performing what ought to be done and refraining from what ought not to be done—as the defining mark of noble conduct.
  - Moral accountability: The Mahabharata and Manusmriti associate the same pairing of commission and omission with the need for expiation.


### Verse 7

I propose a Vidura Niti verse, Mahabharata Udyoga Parva 5.33 (BORI), as the primary parallel for this kural. The correspondence is particularly close between निश्चित्य यः प्रक्रमते, one who commences an undertaking having first reached a firm decision, and எண்ணித் துணிக கருமம். The subsequent नान्तर्वसति कर्मणः, one who does not hesitate or linger in the midst of an undertaking, resonates with துணிந்தபின் எண்ணுவம்: deliberation and resolution must precede commencement, not become grounds for vacillation once the work is underway. Vidura presents this as the conduct of a wise person, further characterized by अवन्ध्यकालः (one who does not waste time) and वश्यात्मा (one who has control over himself). Valluvar expresses the same injunction from the negative perspective, declaring post-commencement second-guessing to be இழுக்கு, a fault or disgrace.

Panchatantra 5.17 provides a particularly close semantic parallel: अपरीक्ष्य न कर्तव्यं कर्तव्यं सुपरीक्षितम् \| पश्चाद् भवति सन्तापो ब्राह्मणी नकुलं यथा \|\| (One should not undertake an action without examining it; one should act only after thorough examination. Otherwise, remorse follows, as in the case of the Brahmani and the mongoose.) The distinction is that Valluvar identifies the fault in reconsidering an undertaking after committing to it, whereas the Panchatantra emphasizes the remorse resulting from acting without adequate prior examination. The latter uses परीक्ष् (to examine) rather than எண்ணு (to think), but the concepts converge in the requirement for careful deliberation before action. The Panchatantra verse introduces the story of a Brahmin lady who leaves her infant in the care of their pet mongoose. The mongoose kills a snake that threatens the child, but when the mother returns and sees blood on the mongoose, she mistakenly assumes that it has harmed the infant and kills it. Discovering the truth, she is overcome with remorse. The story illustrates the consequences of acting without examining the circumstances. Significantly, the verse belongs to the fifth tantra, Aparikshitakarakam, which deals with actions undertaken without adequate examination. The Panchatantra thus complements Valluvar: the kural identifies post-commitment reconsideration as a fault, while the fable demonstrates the consequences of insufficient deliberation before acting.

Arthashastra 1.15.1–2 corroborates the requirement that deliberation precede the commencement of an undertaking. Kautilya directs the ruler: कृतस्वपक्षपरपक्षोपग्रहः कार्यारम्भांश्चिन्तयेत् \| मन्त्रपूर्वाः सर्वारम्भाः \|\|. The first injunction, कार्यारम्भांश्चिन्तयेत् (one should deliberate on the commencement of undertakings), closely corresponds to எண்ணித் துணிக கருமம். The preceding qualification requires the ruler to have secured his own side and dealt with the opposing side before considering the undertaking. The following declaration, मन्त्रपूर्वाः सर्वारम्भाः (all undertakings must be preceded by counsel), further establishes deliberation as a prerequisite to action. Kautilya thus extends Valluvar's injunction by specifying both the considerations and the consultative process that should precede commencement.

Bharavi's Kiratarjuniyam 2.30 offers a poetic formulation that brings out both the danger of rash action and the benefit of deliberation: सहसा विदधीत न क्रिया-मविवेकः परमापदां पदम् \| वृणते हि विमृश्यकारिणं गुणलुब्धाः स्वयमेव सम्पदः \|\| (One should not undertake an action hastily, for lack of discernment is the source of the greatest calamities. Indeed, prosperity, desirous of virtues, chooses of its own accord the person who acts after due deliberation.) The first half, सहसा विदधीत न क्रियाम् (one should not act hastily), reinforces the injunction எண்ணித் துணிக கருமம், while अविवेकः परमापदां पदम् (thoughtlessness is the source of the greatest calamities) underscores the danger of acting without prior consideration. The second half adds a positive dimension that Valluvar leaves implicit: prosperity itself gravitates toward the person who acts विमृश्य (after due deliberation). Thus, Bharavi not only warns against rash action but also presents thoughtful action as a quality that attracts success.

### Verse 10

I propose Yajnavalkya Smriti 1.155 as the primary parallel for this kural. Yajnavalkya cautions against undertaking even an apparently righteous act when it is contrary to accepted social norms and invites public condemnation. The correspondence is between Valluvar's கொள்ளாத கொள்ளா வுலகு and Yajnavalkya's लोकविद्विष्टम्, that which is disliked or censured by the world. Valluvar's எள்ளாத, conveying conduct that invites public reproach or contempt, is implicit in Yajnavalkya's लोकविद्विष्टम्. The injunction यत्नाद् धर्मं समाचरेत्, to practise dharma diligently, corresponds to செயல்வேண்டும், while எண்ணிச், the deliberation preceding action, is implicit in the qualification that even apparently dharmic conduct must not be undertaken if it is अस्वर्ग्यम् and लोकविद्विष्टम्. Valluvar's கொள்ளாத encompasses actions that the world will not accept, whether or not they are righteous; Yajnavalkya makes this breadth explicit through धर्म्यमपि (even if righteous). Yajnavalkya also articulates both the positive and negative injunctions explicitly through समाचरेत् and आचरेन्न, whereas Valluvar's செயல்வேண்டும் expresses the positive injunction, leaving its negative counterpart implicit. An additional resonance occurs in कर्मणा मनसा वाचा, which evokes the three dimensions of Valluvar's expression: எள்ளாத (relating to speech), எண்ணி (relating to thought), and செயல் (relating to action). While this is not the primary correspondence, the convergence is suggestive: both formulations encompass speech, thought, and action. Despite differences in poetic expression, Yajnavalkya Smriti 1.155 closely matches the crux of the kural.

Valmiki Ramayana, Aranya Kanda 3.28.4 provides poetic corroboration of the consequence implicit in Valluvar's expression: कर्म लोकविरुद्धं तु कुर्वाणं क्षणदाचर \| तीक्ष्णं सर्वजनो हन्ति सर्पं दुष्टमिवागतम् \|\|. (O night-roaming one, everyone destroys a person performing a cruel deed opposed to the world, just as they destroy a wicked serpent that has appeared before them.) Valmiki's लोकविरुद्धम्, conduct opposed to the world, is closely related to Yajaavalkya's लोकविद्विष्टम्. The simile of the serpent being destroyed makes the consequence particularly vivid: the world does not merely disapprove of such conduct but actively rejects its perpetrator. This gives a forceful poetic expression to கொள்ளாத கொள்ளா வுலகு.

Kautilya extends the same principle into the sphere of governance. Arthashastra 1.19.34 establishes the overarching criterion for the ruler: प्रजासुखे सुखं राज्ञः प्रजानां च हिते हितम् \| नात्मप्रियं हितं राज्ञः प्रजानां तु प्रियं हितम् \|\|. The king's happiness lies in the happiness of his subjects, and his welfare in their welfare; what is pleasing to the king is not necessarily beneficial, whereas what is pleasing to the subjects is beneficial to him. The ruler must therefore assess his actions not merely by his own preferences but by their consequences for the people whose welfare sustains his own. Kautilya's प्रजानां तु प्रियं हितम् signifies the positive counterpart of Valluvar's கொள்ளாத கொள்ளா வுலகு. 

Valluvar's injunction to consider what the world will accept or reject finds a specific practical application in Kautilya's treatment of punishment in Arthashastra 1.4.8–12. Kauṭilya observes: तीक्ष्णदण्डो हि भूतानामुद्वेजनीयो भवति (excessively severe punishment causes distress among the people); मृदुदण्डः परिभूयते (excessively mild punishment is disregarded or held in contempt); यथार्हदण्डः पूज्यते (punishment proportionate to the offence is respected); सुविज्ञातप्रणीतो हि दण्डः प्रजा धर्मार्थकामैर्योजयति (properly understood and administered punishment enables the subjects to pursue dharma, artha, and kama). He further warns that improperly administered punishment, whether arising from desire, anger, or ignorance, provokes even forest-dwellers and ascetics, let alone householders: दुष्प्रणीतः कामक्रोधाभ्यामवज्ञानाद् वा वानप्रस्थपरिव्राजकानपि कोपयति, किंऽङ्ग पुनर्गृहस्थान् \|\|. The progression is instructive: excessive severity provokes the people, excessive leniency invites contempt, and proportionate punishment earns respect. Thus, the ruler's actions must be measured against the standards and legitimate expectations of those subject to them.

    • Mahābhārata, Anuśāsana Parva 13.145
        ◦ The Mahābhārata's Anuśāsana Parva (Dāna-dharma) offers a useful ethical corroboration through its elaborate discussion of pātratā (worthiness or suitability of the recipient). It identifies the giver, the recipient, the object given, the manner of giving, and the place and time as the six constituents of a proper gift. Particularly relevant is its insistence that the gift must be suited to the recipient's individual need: अन्नस्य क्षुधितं पात्रं तृषितं तु जलस्य वै — food is appropriate for the hungry, and water for the thirsty. It further states एवं पात्रेषु नानात्वमिष्यते पुरुषं प्रति, recognising that the suitability of recipients differs from person to person. The text also enjoins the giver to ascertain the recipient's pātratā before giving and to bestow the gift in a manner that satisfies the recipient. Thus, the Mahābhārata reinforces the principle that benevolence is not simply a matter of giving what is intrinsically good, but of discerning what is appropriate to the particular recipient. While its immediate concern is the propriety of charitable giving rather than the possible error in conciliatory treatment, it provides a valuable ethical parallel to Valluvar's insistence on understanding அவரவர் பண்பு before undertaking even a well-intentioned act.
