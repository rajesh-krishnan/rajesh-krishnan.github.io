## Comments and Research Notes

### Verse 1

I propose Arthashastra 7.12.31-32 as the primary parallel for this kural. Valluvar's அழிவதூஉம் ஆவதூஉம் finds its direct counterpart in Kautilya's क्षयः वृद्धिः, while the opening अल्पागमातिव्ययता identifies the financial condition that produces decline: inadequate revenue combined with excessive expenditure; the reverse produces growth. This causal formulation illuminates வழிபயக்கும் ஊதியம்: the net return of an undertaking, determined by आय and व्यय, is what may lead either to அழிவு or ஆக்கம். The concluding कर्मसु connects directly with Valluvar's செயல், while ज्ञेयम् supplies the deliberative injunction expressed by சூழ்ந்து: the financial position of the undertaking must be assessed before proceeding. Kautilya specifically requires equal attention to revenue and expenditure, rather than considering either in isolation.

The accounting concept underlying this assessment is further clarified by Arthashastra 2.6.27: संजातादायव्ययविशुद्धा नीवी, प्राप्ता चानुवृत्ता च \|\|. Kautilya defines नीवी as the balance remaining after the reckoning of आय and व्यय, distinguishing between a newly received balance and one carried forward. Valluvar's ஊதியம் may be understood in relation to this difference between revenue and expenditure. This is a conceptual correspondence rather than an assertion of technical identity: the precise accounting treatment of items such as interest on debt, taxes, depreciation, and amortization may differ between the two systems.

The broader statecraft dimension of அழிவு, ஆக்கம், and ஊதியம் emerges from Kautilya's preceding formulation in Arthashastra 7.12.29-30: परकर्मोदयो नेतुः क्षयो वृद्धिर्विपर्यये \| तुल्ये कर्मपथे स्थानं ज्ञेयं स्वं विजिगीषुणा \|\| Here क्षय and वृद्धि describe the decline or advancement of the ruler arising from the outcome of an undertaking, rather than merely a commercial loss or gain. Read in this wider context, Valluvar's அழிவு and ஆக்கம், and the ஊதியம் that may lead to either, need not be confined to monetary consequences. They may encompass the broader consequences and returns of an action for the ruler and the state.


### Verse 2

I propose Mahabharata Udyoga Parva 5.40.21 (BORI) as the primary parallel for this kural. The expression தெரிந்த வினத்தொடு finds a close correspondence in प्रज्ञावृद्धं धर्मवृद्धं विद्यावृद्धं स्वबन्धुम् — a trusted kinsman who is mature in wisdom, dharma, and learning. Likewise, தேர்ந்தெண்ணிச் செய்வார்க்கு corresponds to कार्याकार्ये पूजयित्वा प्रसाद्य यः सम्पृच्छेत्: one who honors, conciliates, and consults such a person concerning what ought and ought not to be done. The concluding न स मुह्येत् कदाचित् — he is never deluded — captures the assurance of அரும்பொருள் யாதொன்றும் இல், although the former expresses freedom from delusion rather than the latter's more affirmative assertion that nothing is difficult or beyond comprehension. The correspondence is therefore not exact in its concluding sense, and the Mahabharata leaves the actual undertaking implicit, but it captures the central idea of deliberating with trusted, learned associates before deciding upon action.

Kalingar's gloss supports this interpretation of தெரிந்த வினத்தொடு as consultation with learned associates. He explains that even a king capable of discerning how an undertaking should be performed ought to consult a learned and good assembly, and then deliberate within his own mind before proceeding: யாதும் ஒரு கருமம் செய்யுமிடத்து அதனைத் தெரிந்து செய்யும் திறமையரேனும் மற்று அதனைக் கல்வியால் தெரிந்த நல்லினத்துடனே உசாவிக்கொண்டு தமது நெஞ்சானும் தெரியத் தேர்ந்துகொண்டு இங்ஙனம் செய்யும் அரசர்க்கு; (உசாவி- வினவியாராய்ந்து). His explanation of உசாவி as வினவியாராய்ந்து is particularly relevant to the deliberative dimension of the kural. The gloss is not entirely unambiguous, however, since it largely repeats the kural's own expression when explaining இனத்தொடு.

A related conception of the qualifications of a counselor appears in Mahabharata Shanti Parva 12.82.3 (BORI): नासुहृत्परमं मन्त्रं नारदार्हति वेदितुम् \| अपण्डितो वापि सुहृत्पण्डितो वापि नात्मवान् \|\|. Neither one who is not a friend, nor a friend who is unlearned, nor even a learned friend who lacks self-control, is fit to know the highest counsel. Its conjunction of friendship, learning, and self-mastery corroborates a reading of தெரிந்த வினம் in which trusted association and intellectual discernment are complementary qualifications.

If தெரிந்த வினத்தொடு is read primarily as learned counsel, without emphasizing the kinship or friendship dimension, Kamandakiya Nitisara 12.8–9 offers another close correspondence: अशिक्षितनयः सिंहो हन्तीभं केवलं बलात् \| तं च धीरो नरस्तेषां शतानि मतिमाञ् जयेत् \|\| पश्यद्भिर्दूरतोऽपायान् सूपायप्रतिपत्तिभिः \| भवन्तीह फलायैव विद्वद्भिर्मन्त्रिताः क्रियाः \|\|. The second verse is especially suggestive: विद्वद्भिर्मन्त्रिताः क्रियाः — undertakings deliberated upon with the learned — फलाय एव भवन्ति, are productive of results. This corresponds closely to தெரிந்த வினத்தொடு தேர்ந்தெண்ணிச் செய்வார்க்கு, with the emphasis on deliberation informed by learned counsel. The preceding verse supplies a complementary illustration: a wise and discerning man can overcome a hundred uneducated lions, whose strength is merely brute force. The contrast between मतिमान् and अशिक्षितनयः suggests that intelligence and discernment can overcome formidable obstacles, offering a metaphorical correspondence to அரும்பொருள் யாதொன்றும் இல். 

Kautilya provides a more systematic corroboration of the deliberative dimension in Arthashastra 1.15.20–21: अनुपलब्धस्य ज्ञानमुपलब्धस्य निश्चितबलाधानमर्थद्वैधस्य संशयच्छेदनमेकदेशदृष्टस्य शेषोपलब्धिरिति मन्त्रिसाध्यमेतत् \| तस्माद् बुद्धिवृद्धैः सार्धमध्यासीत मन्त्रम् \|\|. He identifies the resolution of uncertainty where alternatives present themselves (अर्थद्वैधस्य संशयच्छेदनम्) and the ascertainment of what remains when only part of a matter has been perceived (एकदेशदृष्टस्य शेषोपलब्धिः) as tasks requiring collective counsel (मन्त्रिसाध्यमेतत्). Hence the king should deliberate with those mature in intellect (बुद्धिवृद्धैः सार्धम्). This closely corroborates தேர்ந்தெண்ணிச் செய்வார்க்கு and the learned-associate dimension of தெரிந்த வினத்தொடு. It also reinforces the reasoning underlying the primary parallel: consultation with intellectually mature counselors prevents the king from proceeding on incomplete understanding or unresolved uncertainty.

If இனத்தொடு is instead read as emphasizing the support of friends and associates in carrying out an undertaking, Hitopadesha 1.2 offers a particularly close parallel: असाधना वित्तहीना बुद्धिमन्तः सुहृन्मताः \| साधयन्त्याशु कार्याणि काककूर्ममृगाखुवत् \|\|. Though without resources and bereft of wealth, the intelligent, endowed with friends, quickly accomplish their tasks, as did the crow, the tortoise, the deer, and the mouse. Two expressions deserve particular attention: बुद्धिमन्तः सुहृन्मताः, which combines intelligence with the support of friends, and साधयन्त्याशु कार्याणि, which affirms the swift accomplishment of undertakings. The former is especially interesting in relation to தெரிந்த வினம், since it brings together the intellectual and relational dimensions of the expression. The latter corresponds to the assurance of அரும்பொருள் யாதொன்றும் இல். The verse does not assert that nothing whatsoever is difficult, but demonstrates that even those without material resources can accomplish their undertakings through intelligence and friendship. Therefore it has a correspondence to the kural's overall structure.

Manu Smriti 7.55 supplies the converse proposition: अपि यत् सुकरं कर्म तदप्येकेन दुष्करम् \| विशेषतोऽसहायेन किं तु राज्यं महोदयम् \|\|. Even an easy undertaking becomes difficult for one person acting alone, especially without assistance — how much more so the great undertaking of governing a kingdom! While Manu emphasizes the difficulty of acting alone rather than the efficacy of learned deliberation, the verse corroborates the relational dimension of the kural. The implication is that an undertaking becomes more manageable through the support of others, lending further force to the assertion that no task need remain difficult when undertaken with appropriate associates.

Finally, Valmiki Ramayana, Ayodhya Kanda 30.36 (Gorakhpur or Southern Rescensions have this verse), offers a striking correspondence to the concluding expression: स्वर्गो धनं वा धान्यं वा विद्या पुत्रास्सुखानि च \| गुरुवृत्यानुरोधेन न किञ्चिदपि दुर्लभम् \|\|. Heaven, wealth, grain, learning, children, and happiness — nothing is unattainable through following the instructions of one's gurus. The phrase न किञ्चिदपि दुर्लभम् is remarkably close to அரும்பொருள் யாதொன்றும் இல். The context differs: the Ramayana verse recommends following the conduct and instructions of one's gurus generally, rather than specifically deliberating with learned counselors before undertaking an action.

The parallels illuminate three complementary dimensions of the kural. First, தெரிந்த வினத்தொடு can denote trusted, learned associates with whom one deliberates before acting; Mahabharata 5.40.21 captures this most fully, with Mahabharata 12.82.3 corroborating the conjunction of friendship and learning. Second, it can emphasize deliberation with intellectually mature counselors, as expressed particularly well by Kamandakiya Nitisara 12.9 and Arthashastra 1.15.20–21. Third, இனத்தொடு can be understood relationally as undertaking work with the support of friends rather than alone, a dimension brought out by Hitopadesha 1.2 and Manu Smriti 7.55. The concluding அரும்பொருள் யாதொன்றும் இல் receives its closest verbal correspondence in Ramayana 30.36, न किञ्चिदपि दुर्लभम्, while Kamandakiya Nitisara 12.8 offers a metaphorical expression of the power of wisdom to overcome formidable difficulties. An ideal parallel would unite the learned and trusted associates of Mahabharata 5.40.21, the deliberative process of Kamandakiya Nitisara and Kautilya, and the assurance of न किञ्चिदपि दुर्लभम्. No single verse does so. Nevertheless, Mahabharata 5.40.21 remains the best primary parallel because it captures the central prescription of the kural: sound action proceeds from prior deliberation with trusted and qualified associates.


### Verse 3

I propose Brihaspati Smriti, Rinadana 1.10.40,43 as the primary parallel for this kural. The context of भुङ्क्ते बन्धकं लोभात् — enjoying pledged property out of greed — suggests an undertaking motivated by the prospect of gain (ஆக்கம் கருதி); its consequence, मूलहानिः प्रजायते — results in the loss of the principal — corresponds directly to முதலிழக்கும். The reference to the enjoyment and eventual deterioration of the pledged asset supplies the context for செய்வினை, an undertaking whose apparent benefit comes at the expense of the underlying capital. The limitation is that Brihaspati addresses pledged property specifically, whereas Valluvar generalizes the principle to any undertaking in which anticipated gain imperils the principal.

Kautilya provides a useful economic framework for understanding the distinction between apparent gain and genuine gain in Arthashastra 9.4.5–7: सुप्राप्यानुपाल्यः परेषामप्रत्यादेय इत्यादेयः \| विपर्यये प्रत्यादेयः \| तमाददानस्तत्रस्थो वा विनाशं प्राप्नोति \|\|. A gain that is easily acquired, can be maintained, and need not be returned to another is classified as आदेय (retainable gain). The reverse is प्रत्यादेय (gain liable to be returned). One who takes such a gain, or remains dependent upon it, meets destruction. The distinction is important because an apparent acquisition may in reality constitute a liability whose eventual repayment destroys the recipient. Kautilya's classification helps clarify the economic dimension of முதலிழக்கும்: an anticipated gain cannot be treated as genuine advancement if its acquisition entails the eventual surrender of the underlying capital. This is a conceptual and economic corroboration rather than a direct parallel to Valluvar's injunction.

Yajnavalkya Smriti 2.38, introduces a related consideration: कान्तारगास् तु दशकं सामुद्रा विंशकं शतम् \| दद्युर् वा स्वकृतां वृद्धिं सर्वे सर्वासु जातिषु \|\|. Those who travel through forests shall pay ten (per cent), and those who travel by sea twenty (per cent). Alternatively, all persons, irrespective of caste, shall pay the interest they have agreed upon. The Mitakshara commentary explains the differential interest applicable to those who borrow money for dangerous journeys through forests and across the sea: कान्तारम् अरण्यं तत्र गच्छतीति कान्तारगाः \| ये वृद्ध्या धनं गृहीत्वाधिकलाभार्थम् अतिगहनं प्राणधनविनाशशङ्कास्थानं प्रविशन्ति ते दशकं शतं दद्युः \| ये च समुद्रगास् ते विंशकं शतं मासि मासीत्य् एव \| एतद् उक्तं भवति : कान्तारगेभ्यो दशकं शतं सामुद्रेभ्यश् च विंशकं उत्तमर्ण आदद्यान् मूलविनाशस्यापि शङ्कितत्वाद् इति \|\|. Those who borrow at interest to pursue greater profits by undertaking dangerous journeys through forests or across the sea are charged higher rates of interest, reflecting the risk of loss of both life and capital. The commentary explicitly recognizes the possibility of मूलविनाश — destruction of the principal — as the reason for the higher return demanded by the lender. Although this is not a direct injunction against such undertakings, it corroborates the underlying economic reasoning of the kural: the prospect of greater returns must be weighed against the possibility of losing the principal. It is a useful qualification, since Valluvar does not condemn the pursuit of gain itself, but the pursuit of gain through undertakings that imperil the capital.

Mahabharata Shanti Parva 12.106.15,18 (BORI) offers a particularly interesting illustration of the principle through its deliberate inversion. In the dialogue concerning the Kalakavrikshiya sage, a prince is advised how to undermine a rival ruler by inducing him to undertake costly and unproductive projects: आरम्भांश्चास्य महतो दुष्करांस्त्वं प्रयोजय \| नदीबन्धविरोधांश्च बलवद्भिर्विरुध्यताम् \|\| त्रिविष्टपे पुण्यतमं स्थानं प्राप्नोति पार्थिवः \| कोशक्षये त्वमित्राणां वशं कौसल्य गच्छति \|\|. The advice is to induce the rival king to embark on great and difficult undertakings, engage in costly conflicts over river embankments, and expend his treasury on extravagant projects and pleasures. The intended consequence is expressed in कोशक्षये त्वमित्राणां वशं ... गच्छति — when the treasury is depleted, he falls under the control of his enemies. This is almost an inverted illustration of Valluvar's principle: instead of a wise ruler refusing an undertaking that sacrifices capital for anticipated gain, an adversary deliberately recommends such undertakings to bring about the ruler's financial ruin. The Mahabharata thus demonstrates the strategic consequences of failing to preserve the treasury, although its immediate concern is the deliberate impoverishment of an enemy rather than the prudence of selecting undertakings.

The parallels converge on the distinction between apparent gain and the preservation of underlying capital. Brihaspati Smriti supplies the closest correspondence through मूलहानिः प्रजायते, directly expressing the loss of principal resulting from the pursuit or enjoyment of an asset. Arthashastra 9.4.5–7 provides the conceptual distinction between retainable gain and a gain that carries an eventual liability, while Yajnavalkya Smriti, through the Mitakshara commentary, recognizes the possibility of principal loss as a defining consideration in high-risk undertakings. Mahabharata 12.106.15–18 illustrates the strategic consequences of deliberately inducing a ruler to undertake expenditures that deplete his treasury. Taken together, these texts reinforce the economic prudence underlying the kural: the prospect of advancement cannot justify an undertaking that places the principal at risk. The wise assess not merely what an undertaking may yield, but what it may consume in the process.


### Verse 4

I propose Kamandakiya Nitisara 12.39 as the primary parallel for this kural. தெளிவிலதனை finds correspondence in यत्र मन्त्रिमनःसाम्यम् — where there is agreement among the counselors — and यत्र चेतो न शङ्कते — where the mind itself entertains no doubt. Together, these express both the deliberative assurance arising from counsel and the inward certainty required before commencing an undertaking. இளிவென்னும் ஏதப்பாடு அஞ்சு பவர் finds its correspondence in यत्र सन्तो न निन्दन्ति — where the virtuous do not condemn; while तत् परीयाच् चिकीर्षितम् — one should pursue precisely that undertaking — expresses the positive prescription corresponding to தொடங்கார். The correspondence is particularly close in its combination of considered judgment, freedom from misgiving, and protection from reproach.

Kalingar's gloss reinforces the deliberative interpretation of தெளிதல். He explains that an undertaking should be commenced only after the king and the learned have examined and reached clarity, whether together or separately: இங்ஙனம் தாமும் சான்றோரும் தம்முள் கூடியாயினும் தனித்தாயினும் தெளிந்து, சிறந்த கருமஞ் செய்யும் அத்துணை அல்லது மற்றுந் தெளிவில்லதனைச் செய்யத் தொடங்கார். This is particularly significant because it makes clear that தெளிவு is not simply the ruler's private conviction; it is informed by examination with the learned, while also requiring his own considered judgment.

Kautilya articulates the same principle as an explicit rule of statecraft in Arthashastra 1.15.1–2: कृतस्वपक्षपरपक्षोपग्रहः कार्यारम्भांश्चिन्तयेत् \| मन्त्रपूर्वाः सर्वारम्भाः \|\|. The first sutra directs the ruler, after securing his own side and dealing with the opposing side, to deliberate on the commencement of undertakings. The second states that all undertakings must be preceded by counsel (मन्त्रपूर्वाः सर्वारम्भाः). This is a particularly close corroboration of தொடங்கார்: whereas Valluvar cautions against commencing an undertaking without clarity, Kautilya positively prescribes that every undertaking begin with deliberation. The expression मन्त्रपूर्वाः also provides a concise Sanskrit counterpart to the deliberative dimension of தெளிவு.

Kamandakiya Nitisara 12.11–12 further develops the requirement to examine feasibility before commencement: शक्याशक्यपरिच्छेदं कुर्याद् बुद्ध्या प्रसन्नया \| केवलं दन्तभङ्गाय दन्तिनः शैलताडनम् \|\| अशक्यारम्भवृत्तीनां कुतः क्लेशाद् ऋते फलम् \| आकाशम् आस्वादयतः कुतस् तु कवलग्रहः \|\|. One should carefully distinguish what is feasible from what is infeasible through a clear and discerning intellect. An elephant striking a mountain merely breaks its tusks; those who undertake impossible tasks obtain nothing from their exertions but hardship, just as one who attempts to taste the sky cannot obtain a mouthful. The expression शक्याशक्यपरिच्छेदम् — discerning what is possible and impossible — gives a further dimension to தெளிவிலதனை. The warning अशक्यारम्भवृत्तीनां कुतः क्लेशाद् ऋते फलम् directly explains why an undertaking should not be commenced without such examination: its likely outcome is hardship without fruit. These verses complement the primary parallel by emphasizing practical feasibility, rather than the agreement of counselors and the avoidance of censure.

Kautilya provides a systematic account of the dimensions that such deliberation must cover in Arthashastra 1.15.42: कर्मणामारम्भोपायः पुरुषद्रव्यसम्पद् देशकालविभागो विनिपातप्रतीकारः कार्यसिद्धिरिति पञ्चाङ्गो मन्त्रः \|\|. He identifies five constituents of counsel (पञ्चाङ्गो मन्त्रः): 1. कर्मणामारम्भोपायः — the means of commencing the undertaking; 2. पुरुषद्रव्यसम्पद् — the adequacy of personnel and material resources; 3. देशकालविभागः — consideration of place and time; 4. विनिपातप्रतीकारः — provision for setbacks and contingencies; and 5. कार्यसिद्धिः — successful completion of the undertaking. This enumeration makes explicit what is involved in reaching the clarity required before commencement. It is not merely agreement among counselors or confidence in one's judgment, but an examination of the means, resources, circumstances, risks, and prospects of completion. Kautilya thus supplies the practical and procedural dimensions underlying Valluvar's தெளிதல்.

Finally, Subhashitaratnabhandagara, Sankirnaka Prakarana 7.119, provides a particularly apt corroboration of the consequence of commencing an undertaking without properly assessing one's capabilities: अक्षमः क्षमतामानी क्रियायां यः प्रवर्तते \| स हि हास्यास्पदत्वं च लभते प्राणसंशयम् \|\|. One who is incapable, yet considers himself capable, and proceeds with an undertaking becomes an object of ridicule and even risks his life. The correspondence is especially strong in हास्यास्पदत्वम् — becoming an object of ridicule — which directly illustrates இளிவு. The verse also introduces an important dimension of தெளிதல்: an honest assessment of one's own competence. Where the Kamandakiya Nitisara verses emphasize the feasibility of the undertaking, this subhashita emphasizes the fitness of the person undertaking it. Both are necessary considerations before commencement.

The parallels illuminate three complementary aspects of the kural. Kamandakiya Nitisara 12.39 captures the central prescription: an undertaking should be pursued only when there is agreement among counselors, no inward misgiving, and no prospect of censure from the virtuous. Kautilya's मन्त्रपूर्वाः सर्वारम्भाः establishes deliberation as a prerequisite to all undertakings, while his five constituents of counsel enumerate what that examination entails. Kamandakiya Nitisara 12.11–12 adds the assessment of feasibility, and Subhashitaratnabhanḍagara 119 highlights the danger and ridicule that follow when one overestimates one's own competence. Together, these texts show that தெளிதல் is not merely confidence in a proposed action, but a considered judgment of its feasibility, the adequacy of the means and the person undertaking it, and its likely consequences. The fear of இளிவு is thus not simply fear of public ridicule; it is the prudent avoidance of undertakings that cannot withstand either rational examination or the judgment of the discerning.


### Verse 5

I propose Mahabharata Sabha Parva 2.5.47 (BORI) as the primary parallel for this kural. The Sanskrit verse asks whether the king advances against an enemy only after examining the threefold strength (समीक्ष्य त्रिविधं बलम्), whereas the kural warns against advancing without such examination (வகையறச் சூழா). The correspondence is further seen in எழுதல் and अभियासि जवेनैव, setting out against the enemy with haste, and பகைவரைப் and शत्रुम्, the enemy against whom the advance is directed. Thus, the Sanskrit articulates the precaution positively, while the kural states the danger through its negation. 

While பாத்திப் படுப்பது is not explicitly stated in this verse, it is implied by the context. The surrounding discussion elaborates the preparations necessary for a sound military advance, including assessing the enemy's position, securing one's own forces, cultivating support within the enemy's camp, and ensuring a secure base. An ill-considered advance may therefore squander one's resources and give the enemy an opportunity to recover and grow stronger, providing an interpretive connection to பாத்திப் படுப்பது.

Nilakantha's commentary on त्रिविधं बलम् (verse 2.5.57, which differs in numbering from the BORI edition) offers two interpretations. The first is मन्त्रकोशभृत्यबलानि, comprising the strength of counsel and strategic intelligence (मन्त्र), the treasury and material resources (कोश), and servants, retainers, and military personnel (भृत्य). Alternatively, he interprets the expression as प्रभु, मन्त्र, उत्साह — the three powers of sovereignty and material resources, counsel and strategic intelligence, and energy and personal enterprise. The two interpretations together illuminate the breadth of வகையறச் சூழுதல்: an assessment of the resources, counsel, personnel, and the capacity and resolve required to undertake the action successfully.

Valmiki Ramayana, Yuddha Kanda 6.104.18-19 (in some rescensions), offers an even broader account of what such an assessment entails: देशकालौ च विज्ञेयौ लक्षणान्यिङ्गितानि च \| दैन्यं हर्षश्च खेदश्च रथिनश्च बलाबलम् \|\| स्थलनिम्नानि भूमेश्च समानि विषमाणि च \| युद्धकालश्च विज्ञेयः परस्यान्तरदर्शनम् \|\| The considerations include place and time (देशकालौ), signs and indications (लक्षणानि, इङ्गितानि), the morale and fatigue of the warriors, relative strengths and weaknesses (बलाबलम्), the character of the terrain, and opportunities arising from the enemy's vulnerabilities (परस्यान्तरदर्शनम्). This is a useful elaboration of வகையறச் சூழுதல்: the assessment must be comprehensive, taking account of the enemy, one's own forces, the terrain, and the circumstances governing the timing of action.

A further, particularly interesting connection to பாத்திப் படுப்பது occurs in Mahabharata Sabha Parva 2.50.23–24 (BORI), where Duryodhana describes the danger of allowing a prospering enemy faction to grow unchecked: शत्रुपक्षं समृध्यन्तं यो मोहात्समुपेक्षते \| व्याधिराप्यायित इव तस्य मूलं छिनत्ति सः \|\| अल्पोऽपि ह्यरिरत्यन्तं वर्धमानपराक्रमः \| वल्मीको मूलज इव ग्रसते वृक्षमन्तिकात् \|\|. The person who, through delusion, neglects an enemy faction that is prospering will have his foundations cut away by it, just as by a disease that has been allowed to thrive. The expression व्याधिराप्यायितः is especially suggestive: an enemy, like a disease, becomes more dangerous when allowed to grow. Even a seemingly insignificant adversary, if his strength continues to increase, can eventually consume a powerful opponent, like a termite mound destroying a tree from its roots. These are Duryodhana's words, spoken in the context of his jealousy toward the Pandavas, rather than an independent statement of the epic's ethical position. Nevertheless, the imagery is valuable for understanding the kural's closing expression: failure to act with proper judgment can itself become a means of nurturing the enemy.

Arthashastra 9.1.1 provides a particularly close corroboration of the principle: विजिगीषुरात्मनः परस्य च बलाबलं शक्तिदेशकालयात्राकालबलसमुद्दानकालपश्चात्कोपक्षयव्ययलाभापदां ज्ञात्वा विशिष्टबलो यायात्, अन्यथाऽऽसीत \| Kautilya prescribes that a king contemplating an expedition must first ascertain his own and the enemy's relative strengths and weaknesses, the suitability of power, place and time, and the prospective losses, expenses, gains, and dangers. Only when his own strength is superior should he march; otherwise, he should remain still. This is an especially comprehensive exposition of வகையறச் சூழாது எழுதல்: the assessment must extend beyond the opposing forces to the entire strategic and material circumstances of the proposed undertaking.

Panchatantra, Mitrabheda 1.337, expresses the danger more directly through an illustrative maxim: अन्यच्च- शत्रोर्विक्रममज्ञात्वा वैरमारभते हि यः \| स पराभवमाप्नोति समुद्रष् टिट्टिभाद्यथा \|\|. Whoever begins hostilities without knowing the enemy's strength meets with defeat, as did the tiny tittiibha bird in its conflict with the ocean. The emphasis here is on the consequence of underestimating an adversary, corroborating the relationship between inadequate assessment and the danger inherent in undertaking action.

I read Valluvar's வகையறச் சூழாது எழுதல் primarily in the context of statecraft and warfare, as a caution against undertaking action against an adversary without adequate deliberation. What Valluvar leaves cryptic in வகையறச் சூழுதல் — the range of considerations to be examined before acting — is illuminated by the Mahabharata's त्रिविधं बलम् and the Ramayana's more extensive enumeration of the factors governing a military engagement. Valluvar's warning that rash action may cause the enemy to grow, as though one were sowing and watering a crop, finds a striking metaphorical parallel in Duryodhana's comparison of a neglected, prospering enemy to a disease allowed to spread unchecked. The Arthashastra and the Panchatantra both offer direct practical injunctions against engaging an enemy without first ascertaining his strength and the circumstances governing the undertaking.


### Verse 6

I propose Arthashastra 7.5.21 and 7.5.26 as the primary parallels for this kural. The correspondence in 7.5.21 is particularly close: செய்தக்க வல்ல செயக்கெடும் finds its counterpart in अकार्याणां च करणैः (doing what ought not to be done), while செய்தக்க செய்யாமை யானுங் matches कार्याणां च प्रणाशनैः (frustrating what ought to be done). Even Valluvar's யானுங் finds an echo in Kautilya's च, reinforcing the pairing of the two forms of misconduct. The consequences captured by கெடும் are developed in 7.5.26: योगक्षेमवधेन (through the failure to secure the welfare and security of the subjects) leads to क्षयः, लोभः, and विराग्यम् (impoverishment, greed, and disaffection). 

The significance of this correspondence becomes clearer from the larger passage. Arthashastra 7.5.19–25 develops the principle through a series of paired forms of misgovernance. These include insulting the good and favoring the wicked; permitting unjustified violence; abandoning righteous customs and neglecting dharma; doing what ought not to be done and frustrating what ought to be done; withholding what is due and exacting what is not due; failing to punish the guilty and punishing the innocent; arresting those who should not be seized and releasing those who should be apprehended; undertaking unprofitable works and frustrating profitable ones; failing to protect the people from thieves and robbing them oneself; abandoning enterprise and condemning good works; harming leaders and dishonoring the worthy; provoking elders and acting with partiality and falsehood; failing to remedy wrongs; and neglecting work already undertaken. The passage thus elaborates Valluvar's compact pairing of wrongful action and omission across the full range of royal administration.

From Arthashastra 7.5.26 onward, Kautilya turns to the consequences of such misgovernance. Through the king's negligence, indolence, and failure to secure the welfare and security of his subjects, impoverishment, greed, and disaffection arise. The progression is particularly significant: the impoverished become greedy, the greedy become disaffected, and the disaffected may defect to the enemy or turn against their own ruler. The king must therefore prevent these conditions from arising or remedy them without delay. This gives a distinctly political dimension to Valluvar's கெடும்: the consequences of doing what ought not to be done, or neglecting what ought to be done, extend beyond the immediate undertaking to the deterioration of the kingdom and the ruler's own position.

Yoga Vasistha 6.126.54 provides a positive ethical and spiritual counterpart: कर्तव्यमाचरन्काममकर्तव्यमनाचरन् \| तिष्ठति प्राकृताचारो यः स आर्य इति स्मृतः \|\| (One who willingly performs what ought to be done and refrains from what ought not to be done, living according to proper conduct, is regarded as noble.) The pairing कर्तव्यमाचरन् and अकर्तव्यमनाचरन् directly echoes the two complementary obligations in Valluvar's formulation (செய்தக்க வல்ல and செய்தக்க செய்யாமை). The distinction is in emphasis: the kural warns of the consequences of violating these obligations, whereas the Yoga Vasistha presents their observance as the mark of noble conduct, particularly in the spiritual pursuit.

Mahabharata, Shanti Parva 12.35.2 (BORI) offers another corroboration, extending the consequences from worldly deterioration to moral accountability. Vyasa declares: अकुर्वन्विहितं कर्म प्रतिषिद्धानि चाचरन् \| प्रायश्चित्तीयते ह्येवं नरो मिथ्या च वर्तयन् \|\|. (A person becomes liable to expiation by failing to perform prescribed duties, engaging in prohibited acts, and conducting himself improperly.) The pairing of अकुर्वन् विहितं कर्म (failing to perform prescribed action) and प्रतिषिद्धानि चाचरन् (performing prohibited actions) closely corresponds to Valluvar's twofold formulation. The Mahabharata goes further by explicitly connecting such conduct with प्रायश्चित्त, the need for expiation.

Manusmriti 11.44 reinforces the same connection: अकुर्वन् विहितं कर्म निन्दितं च समाचरन् \| प्रसक्तश्चेन्द्रियार्थेषु प्रायश्चित्तीयते नरः \|\|. (Failing to perform prescribed duties, performing censured acts, and becoming attached to sense-objects make a person liable to expiation.) The correspondence with the Mahabharata is particularly close in the shared expressions अकुर्वन् विहितं कर्म and प्रायश्चित्तीयते. Manusmriti thus corroborates both the prescribed-action/prohibited-action pairing and its connection with expiation, while adding attachment to sense-objects as a further cause of transgression.

A concluding observation: The parallels reveal three complementary dimensions of Valluvar's compact formulation:

  - Statecraft: Kautilya demonstrates how wrongful action and neglect undermine the ruler, the subjects, and the kingdom.
  - Ethical ideal: The Yoga Vasistha presents the converse, performing what ought to be done and refraining from what ought not to be done, as the defining mark of noble conduct.
  - Moral accountability: The Mahabharata and Manusmriti associate the same pairing of commission and omission with the need for expiation.


### Verse 7

I propose a Vidura Niti verse, Mahabharata Udyoga Parva 5.33.24 (BORI), as the primary parallel for this kural. The correspondence is particularly close between निश्चित्य यः प्रक्रमते, one who commences an undertaking having first reached a firm decision, and எண்ணித் துணிக கருமம். The subsequent नान्तर्वसति कर्मणः, one who does not hesitate or linger in the midst of an undertaking, resonates with துணிந்தபின் எண்ணுவம்: deliberation and resolution must precede commencement, not become grounds for vacillation once the work is underway. Vidura presents this as the conduct of a wise person, further characterized by अवन्ध्यकालः (one who does not waste time) and वश्यात्मा (one who has control over himself). Valluvar expresses the same injunction from the negative perspective, declaring post-commencement second-guessing to be இழுக்கு, a fault or disgrace.

Panchatantra 5.17 provides a particularly close semantic parallel: अपरीक्ष्य न कर्तव्यं कर्तव्यं सुपरीक्षितम् \| पश्चाद् भवति सन्तापो ब्राह्मणी नकुलं यथा \|\| (One should not undertake an action without examining it; one should act only after thorough examination. Otherwise, remorse follows, as in the case of the Brahmani and the mongoose.) The distinction is that Valluvar identifies the fault in reconsidering an undertaking after committing to it, whereas the Panchatantra emphasizes the remorse resulting from acting without adequate prior examination. The latter uses परीक्ष् (to examine) rather than எண்ணு (to think), but the concepts converge in the requirement for careful deliberation before action. The Panchatantra verse introduces the story of a Brahmin lady who leaves her infant in the care of their pet mongoose. The mongoose kills a snake that threatens the child, but when the mother returns and sees blood on the mongoose, she mistakenly assumes that it has harmed the infant and kills it. Discovering the truth, she is overcome with remorse. The story illustrates the consequences of acting without examining the circumstances. Significantly, the verse belongs to the fifth tantra, Aparikshitakarakam, which deals with actions undertaken without adequate examination. The Panchatantra thus complements Valluvar: the kural identifies post-commitment reconsideration as a fault, while the fable demonstrates the consequences of insufficient deliberation before acting.

Arthashastra 1.15.1–2 corroborates the requirement that deliberation precede the commencement of an undertaking. Kautilya directs the ruler: कृतस्वपक्षपरपक्षोपग्रहः कार्यारम्भांश्चिन्तयेत् \| मन्त्रपूर्वाः सर्वारम्भाः \|\|. The first injunction, कार्यारम्भांश्चिन्तयेत् (one should deliberate on the commencement of undertakings), closely corresponds to எண்ணித் துணிக கருமம். The preceding qualification requires the ruler to have secured his own side and dealt with the opposing side before considering the undertaking. The following declaration, मन्त्रपूर्वाः सर्वारम्भाः (all undertakings must be preceded by counsel), further establishes deliberation as a prerequisite to action. Kautilya thus extends Valluvar's injunction by specifying both the considerations and the consultative process that should precede commencement.

Bharavi's Kiratarjuniyam 2.30 offers a poetic formulation that brings out both the danger of rash action and the benefit of deliberation: सहसा विदधीत न क्रिया-मविवेकः परमापदां पदम् \| वृणते हि विमृश्यकारिणं गुणलुब्धाः स्वयमेव सम्पदः \|\| (One should not undertake an action hastily, for lack of discernment is the source of the greatest calamities. Indeed, prosperity, desirous of virtues, chooses of its own accord the person who acts after due deliberation.) The first half, सहसा विदधीत न क्रियाम् (one should not act hastily), reinforces the injunction எண்ணித் துணிக கருமம், while अविवेकः परमापदां पदम् (thoughtlessness is the source of the greatest calamities) underscores the danger of acting without prior consideration. The second half adds a positive dimension that Valluvar leaves implicit: prosperity itself gravitates toward the person who acts विमृश्य (after due deliberation). Thus, Bharavi not only warns against rash action but also presents thoughtful action as a quality that attracts success.


### Verse 8

I propose Mahabharata Shanti Parva 12.91.7–8 (BORI) as the primary parallel for this kural. The expression ஆற்றின் வருந்தா corresponds to अधर्मे वर्तमानानाम्, pursuing a course contrary to dharma, while வருத்தம் finds its correspondence in अर्थसिद्धिः प्रदृश्यते, the apparent attainment of material success. The next verse describes how பலர் நின்று போற்றுதல் can follow from this apparent success: तदेव मङ्गलं सर्वं लोकः समनुवर्तते, the world follows that very thing, regarding it as auspicious. The consequence, பொத்துப் படும், finds a particularly suggestive correspondence in उच्छिद्यते धर्मवृत्तम्, the destruction of the established order of dharma, followed by अधर्मो वर्तते महान्, the ascendancy of adharma. 

The correspondence also preserves an important distinction. The Mahabharata describes the social consequences of allowing an unrighteous course to prevail: apparent material success is mistaken for auspiciousness, society follows it, and established dharma is displaced. Valluvar compresses this sequence into the paradox of an undertaking that remains faulty despite the support and approbation of many.

#### Corroborating parallels

Kautilya's Arthashastra 8.2.11–12 sharpens the distinction between the support available to a ruler and the consequences of his chosen course. Kautilya observes: अन्धो राजा शक्यते सहायसम्पदा यत्र तत्र वा पर्यवस्थापयितुम् \| चलितशास्त्रस्तु शास्त्रादन्यथाभिनिविष्टबुद्धिरन्यायेन राज्यं आत्मानं चोपहन्ति \|\|. An ignorant or blind king can be kept on course through the resources and support of his associates (सहायसम्पदा); but a ruler who has deviated from the science of statecraft and whose mind is fixed on a contrary course destroys both his kingdom and himself through unjust conduct (अन्यायेन ... उपहन्ति). This is a particularly useful qualification of பலர்நின்று போற்றினும்: the support of others may sustain a ruler who lacks knowledge, but it cannot redeem one who deliberately persists in an improper course. The correspondence with பொத்துப் படும் is also direct in उपहन्ति, which conveys injury and destruction.

Mahabharata Udyoga Parva 5.34.6–7 (BORI) provides an important distinction between the apparent success of improperly conceived undertakings and the possible failure of properly conducted ones: मिथ्योपेतानि कर्माणि सिध्येयुर्यानि भारत \| अनुपायप्रयुक्तानि मा स्म तेषु मनः कृथाः \|\| तथैव योगविहितं न सिध्येत्कर्म यन्नृप \| उपाययुक्तो मेधावी न तत्र ग्लपयेन्मनः \|\|. Vidura cautions that undertakings founded on improper or deceptive means may nevertheless succeed, and advises against setting one's mind on such undertakings. Conversely, an undertaking properly conceived and equipped with appropriate means may still fail; the wise person should not become disheartened on that account. The first verse is particularly relevant to வருத்தம் and பொத்துப் படும்: apparent success does not validate an undertaking founded on मिथ्योपेतानि or अनुपायप्रयुक्तानि. The second adds an important qualification: failure of an appropriately conducted undertaking does not itself establish that the undertaking was faulty. The expression उपाययुक्तो मेधावी reinforces the importance of sound means and judgment, independently of the eventual outcome.

Mahabharata, Shanti Parva 12.97.2 offers a concise statement of the consequence of adharmic success: अधर्मयुक्तो विजयो ह्यध्रुवोऽस्वर्ग्य एव च \| सादयत्येष राजानं महीं च भरतर्षभ \|\|. Victory obtained through adharma is unstable (अध्रुवः), brings no auspicious or higher merit (अस्वर्ग्यः), and brings down both the king and his kingdom (सादयत्येष राजानं महीं च). This is a particularly close corroboration of பொத்துப் படும், extending the consequence beyond the failure of an individual undertaking to the downfall of the ruler and the realm. It also reinforces the distinction in the primary parallel between apparent material success and the eventual destruction that follows an unrighteous course.

#### The layered interpretations of the kural

The correspondence also benefits from retaining the semantic range of three expressions rather than restricting them to a single interpretation. ஆற்றின் வருந்தா can describe an undertaking contrary to dharma, contrary to sound principles of statecraft, or both. வருத்தம் can denote the undertaking or effort itself, its apparent material success, or the distress arising from its failure. பலர்நின்று போற்றினும் can encompass public praise and endorsement following apparent success, the wider acceptance and imitation of an established practice, or the support of associates and advisers even before the undertaking begins. Finally, பொத்துப் படும் can indicate the failure of the undertaking, the destruction of dharma despite apparent worldly success, or the intrinsic moral failure of the course itself.

The primary Mahabharata parallel captures the social and moral dimensions; Kautilya illustrates the limits of support when a ruler persists in unjust conduct; and Vidura distinguishes success achieved through improper means from failure despite proper means. The additional passage on adharmic victory extends the consequences to the downfall of ruler and kingdom.

### Verse 9

I propose Arthashastra 2.10.41 as the primary parallel for this kural. The expression நன்றாற்றல் corresponds to पूजा, the bestowal of honour or favour, while அவரவர் பண்பறிந்து ஆற்றல் finds a close correspondence in यथार्हगुणसंयुक्ता — the favour being conjoined with, and determined by, the recipient's deserving qualities. Kautilya further identifies assistance in distress and gifts, अप्याधौ परिदाने वा, as forms of such favour (उपग्रहौ). Thus, the correspondence is not simply in the act of beneficence, but in its being calibrated to the particular recipient's merits. Kautilya states this as a positive injunction: favor should be bestowed in accordance with the recipient's deserving qualities. Valluvar expresses the converse as a caution: even a good act carries the potential for error when undertaken without first discerning அவரவர் பண்பு.

The following verse, Arthashastra 2.10.42, extends the principle of differentiated royal beneficence to particular communities, villages, and regions: जातेर्विशेषेषु परेषु चैव ग्रामेषु देशेषु च तेषु तेषु \| अनुग्रहो यो नृपतेर्निदेशात्तज्ज्ञः परिहार इति व्यवस्येत् \|\|. Although this concerns the king's grants or remissions rather than the assessment of an individual's qualities, it reinforces the broader principle that royal beneficence must be adapted to its recipient and circumstances.

In the context of statecraft, நன்றாற்றல் can be understood to encompass the upayas of sama (conciliation) and upapradana (material inducement or reward, corresponding broadly to dana in Niti literature). Arthashastra 2.10.48 enumerates five forms of sama: गुणसंकीर्तनम् (recognition and praise of the recipient's good qualities), सम्बन्धोपाख्यानम् (recalling or invoking relationships), परस्परोपकारसंदर्शनम् (demonstrating mutual benefit), आयतिप्रदर्शनम् (showing prospective benefits), and आत्मोपनिधानम् (offering oneself or placing oneself at the other's disposal). Of particular relevance is गुणसंकीर्तनम्, which makes the recognition of the recipient's qualities an explicit constituent of conciliatory conduct. Arthashastra 2.10.54 defines उपप्रदानम् as अर्थोपकारः, material assistance or the conferral of material benefit. Thus, both conciliatory speech and material rewards fall within the scope of நன்றாற்றல். This interpretation is corroborated by Parimelalagar, who explains: வேந்தர்மாட்டு நன்றான உபாயம் செய்தற்கண்ணும் குற்றம் உண்டாம்; நன்றான உபாயமாவது: கொடுத்தலும் இன்சொல் சொல்லுதலுமாம். He explicitly identifies giving and pleasant speech as forms of beneficial royal conduct, while observing that even these can become faulty when applied without due discrimination.

Arthashastra 7.8.17–19 provides a particularly pertinent statecraft illustration. Kautilya considers two rival kings, the conqueror and his enemy, each extending assistance to the same prospective ally.  He observes: तयोरेकपुरुषानुग्रहे यो मित्रं मित्रतरं वानुगृह्णाति सोऽतिसन्धत्ते \|\| मित्रादात्मवृद्धिं हि प्राप्नोति क्षयव्ययप्रवासपरोपकारानितरः \|\| कृतार्थश्च शत्रुर्वैगुण्यमेति \|\|. Of the two kings assisting the same person, the one who supports the truer or more loyal friend outmaneuvers the other. Through his friend, he gains strength; the other, however, incurs depletion, expenditure and the hardships of campaigning abroad, while conferring benefits upon an enemy. The enemy, having obtained what he sought, is thereby placed in a stronger position. Kautilya thus illustrates how a misjudgment of the recipient's disposition can turn assistance into an instrument of one's own disadvantage. The choice of whom to assist must turn on the recipient's actual disposition and loyalty is established in the preceding discussion in verses 7.8.11–16. This provides a statecraft dimension to Valluvar's தவறுண்டு: failure to discern அவரவர் பண்பு is not merely an error in choosing the appropriate form of beneficence; it can be an error in judging the recipient's intentions and loyalties, whereby the benefactor may unwittingly enrich and strengthen an adversary.

The negative counterpart to the kural is particularly well expressed in Bhavishya Purana 1.189.1, 4. The first verse compares an unsuitable gift to good seed sown in barren soil: जपोपेक्षे तथा दानं सह पात्रेण नश्यति \| सद्बीजमूषरे यद्वत्समुप्तं निष्फलं भवेत् \|\|, that is, the gift perishes along with an unworthy recipient, just as good seed sown in barren ground yields no fruit. The fourth verse sharpens the warning: लोहोडुपेन प्रतरन्निमज्जत्युदके यथा \| दाता दाता ग्रहीता च पतत्यंधे तमस्यथ \|\|, that is, just as one attempting to cross water in a boat of iron sinks, both giver and recipient fall into the darkness. These verses illuminate both senses of Valluvar's தவறுண்டு: the gift becomes fruitless, like good seed sown in barren soil, and the act brings adverse consequences for both giver and recipient, who sink into darkness together. The latter warning is conveyed through the striking simile of a boat made of iron: an instrument intended to facilitate passage across water becomes an impediment to survival, sinking under its own weight. Both images underscore the danger of acting without first discerning அவரவர் பண்பறிந்தாற்றாக் கடை.

A broader ethical corroboration comes from the Mahabharata, Anushasana Parva, Dana-dharma Parva (Gita Press, Gorakhpur, Volume 6, Chapter 234), in its discussion of dana and patrata (the worthiness or suitability of the recipient). The text identifies six constituents of proper giving (13.234.4): data (the giver), pratigrahita (the recipient), deya (the object given), sopakrama (the undertaking or act associated with the giving), desha (place), and kala (time). Particularly relevant is its recognition that the suitability of a gift must be determined by the recipient's need: अन्नस्य क्षुधितं पात्रं तृषितं तु जलस्य वै (13.234.9) — food is appropriate for the hungry, and water for the thirsty. It further observes, एवं पात्रेषु नानात्वमिष्यते पुरुषं प्रति (13.234.9), that the suitability of recipients differs from person to person. The preceding discussion also emphasizes that a recipient must be qualified for the particular gift being offered. While the immediate subject is charitable giving rather than the possible fault in conciliatory treatment, the underlying principle closely accords with அவரவர் பண்பறிந்து: the goodness of an act of giving depends not merely on what is given, but also on discerning to whom it is given and what is appropriate for that recipient.

Thus, in the compact pairing of நன்றாற்றல் and தவறுண்டு, Valluvar captures both the range of benevolent statecraft—from conciliatory speech and material assistance to the conferral of favors—and the danger of misjudging the recipient, whereby a well-intentioned act may prove fruitless, harmful to both parties, or even strengthen an adversary.


### Verse 10

I propose Yajnavalkya Smriti 1.155 as the primary parallel for this kural. Yajnavalkya cautions against undertaking even an apparently righteous act when it is contrary to accepted social norms and invites public condemnation. The correspondence is between Valluvar's கொள்ளாத கொள்ளா வுலகு and Yajnavalkya's लोकविद्विष्टम्, that which is disliked or censured by the world. Valluvar's எள்ளாத, conveying conduct that invites public reproach or contempt, is implicit in Yajnavalkya's लोकविद्विष्टम्. The injunction यत्नाद् धर्मं समाचरेत्, to practise dharma diligently, corresponds to செயல்வேண்டும், while எண்ணிச், the deliberation preceding action, is implicit in the qualification that even apparently dharmic conduct must not be undertaken if it is अस्वर्ग्यम् and लोकविद्विष्टम्. Valluvar's கொள்ளாத encompasses actions that the world will not accept, whether or not they are righteous; Yajnavalkya makes this breadth explicit through धर्म्यमपि (even if righteous). Yajnavalkya also articulates both the positive and negative injunctions explicitly through समाचरेत् and आचरेन्न, whereas Valluvar's செயல்வேண்டும் expresses the positive injunction, leaving its negative counterpart implicit. An additional resonance occurs in कर्मणा मनसा वाचा, which evokes the three dimensions of Valluvar's expression: எள்ளாத (relating to speech), எண்ணி (relating to thought), and செயல் (relating to action). While this is not the primary correspondence, the convergence is suggestive: both formulations encompass speech, thought, and action. Despite differences in poetic expression, Yajnavalkya Smriti 1.155 closely matches the crux of the kural.

Valmiki Ramayana, Aranya Kanda 3.28.4 provides poetic corroboration of the consequence implicit in Valluvar's expression: कर्म लोकविरुद्धं तु कुर्वाणं क्षणदाचर \| तीक्ष्णं सर्वजनो हन्ति सर्पं दुष्टमिवागतम् \|\|. (O night-roaming one, everyone destroys a person performing a cruel deed opposed to the world, just as they destroy a wicked serpent that has appeared before them.) Valmiki's लोकविरुद्धम्, conduct opposed to the world, is closely related to Yajnavalkya's लोकविद्विष्टम्. The simile of the serpent being destroyed makes the consequence particularly vivid: the world does not merely disapprove of such conduct but actively rejects its perpetrator. This gives a forceful poetic expression to கொள்ளாத கொள்ளா வுலகு.

Kautilya extends the same principle into the sphere of governance. Arthashastra 1.19.34 establishes the overarching criterion for the ruler: प्रजासुखे सुखं राज्ञः प्रजानां च हिते हितम् \| नात्मप्रियं हितं राज्ञः प्रजानां तु प्रियं हितम् \|\|. The king's happiness lies in the happiness of his subjects, and his welfare in their welfare; what is pleasing to the king is not necessarily beneficial, whereas what is pleasing to the subjects is beneficial to him. The ruler must therefore assess his actions not merely by his own preferences but by their consequences for the people whose welfare sustains his own. Kautilya's प्रजानां तु प्रियं हितम् signifies the positive counterpart of Valluvar's கொள்ளாத கொள்ளா வுலகு. 

Valluvar's injunction to consider what the world will accept or reject finds a specific practical application in Kautilya's treatment of punishment in Arthashastra 1.4.8–12. Kautilya observes: तीक्ष्णदण्डो हि भूतानामुद्वेजनीयो भवति (excessively severe punishment causes distress among the people); मृदुदण्डः परिभूयते (excessively mild punishment is disregarded or held in contempt); यथार्हदण्डः पूज्यते (punishment proportionate to the offence is respected); सुविज्ञातप्रणीतो हि दण्डः प्रजा धर्मार्थकामैर्योजयति (properly understood and administered punishment enables the subjects to pursue dharma, artha, and kama). He further warns that improperly administered punishment, whether arising from desire, anger, or ignorance, provokes even forest-dwellers and ascetics, let alone householders: दुष्प्रणीतः कामक्रोधाभ्यामवज्ञानाद् वा वानप्रस्थपरिव्राजकानपि कोपयति, किंऽङ्ग पुनर्गृहस्थान् \|\|. The progression is instructive: excessive severity provokes the people, excessive leniency invites contempt, and proportionate punishment earns respect. Thus, the ruler's actions must be measured against the standards and legitimate expectations of those subject to them. 


### Chapter Summary

Although this chapter, தெரிந்து செயல்வகை, is situated within அரசியல், its scope is not confined to the conduct of royal or political undertakings. Its central discipline of discerning, deliberating upon, and wisely executing action has much broader application to human affairs. The principles of this chapter span three overlapping domains: (i) right living, including ethics, spirituality, charity, and general prudence; (ii) statecraft, including governance, counsel, and warfare; and (iii) business and finance, including the assessment of risk, resources, and returns. The ruler's immediate responsibilities are primarily those of statecraft, but their proper discharge requires ethical judgment and financial prudence; conversely, the principles of deliberation and discernment that govern royal undertakings apply equally to individuals in their own affairs.

The ten kurals of this chapter present a comprehensive framework for discerning, deliberating upon, and undertaking action. They address the assessment of an undertaking and its prospective consequences, the choice of appropriate counsel and capable agents, the consideration of means and circumstances before proceeding, and the judgment required in deciding when and how to act, to refrain from acting, or to extend assistance. The Arthashastra offers a particularly close and systematic set of parallels that brings these concerns into sharper focus. It elaborates the assessment of prospective returns and risks, the adequacy of resources, and the competence of those entrusted with execution (7.12.29–32; 2.6.27); the role of qualified counsel and the systematic examination of means, personnel, material resources, place, time, contingencies, and the likelihood of successful completion (1.15.1–2, 1.15.20–21, 1.15.42); and the distinction between appropriate action, wrongful action, and neglect (7.5.19–26). It further extends the discussion to the assessment of an adversary before undertaking a campaign (9.1.1), and to the exercise of beneficence and punishment with due regard to individual qualities and the welfare of subjects (2.10.41–54; 1.4.8–12; 1.19.34). The Mahabharata and Kamandakiya Nitisara underscore the importance of trusted associates and learned counsel in deliberation, while Brihaspati Smriti and Yajnavalkya Smriti bring out its implications for financial prudence and ethical conduct. The Mahabharata and Ramayana elaborate the considerations that must inform military action, extending the assessment beyond an adversary's strength to the circumstances in which an undertaking is pursued. In distinct ways, Kautilya and Vidura Niti illuminate the difference between apparent success and sound action, as well as the consequences of conduct that runs counter to dharma. Together, these parallels place the kurals' concise prescriptions within a wider tradition of practical, ethical, and political reasoning.

The comparisons also reveal the interpretive depth of Valluvar's cryptic expressions. The parallels to நன்றாற்ற லுள்ளுந் are particularly illuminating: the Bhavishya Purana and Mahabharata address the suitability of recipients in charitable giving, while Kautilya extends the principle to political assistance, where a failure to discern an ally's disposition and loyalty may strengthen an adversary. தெரிந்த வினத்தொடு can be understood more broadly, encompassing not only learned counselors but also trusted associates and those whose practical support can be relied upon. வகையறச் சூழுதல், rather than referring merely to a calculation of an adversary's strength, calls for a comprehensive assessment of resources, personnel, terrain, timing, morale, and vulnerabilities. The expression ஆற்றின் வருந்தா வருத்தம், meanwhile, invites reflection on the contrast between an undertaking that enjoys widespread approval and one that is nevertheless unsound and ultimately ruinous. The parallels help distinguish public approbation and apparent success from the ethical and practical soundness of an action. In these ways, the Sanskrit texts do more than corroborate Valluvar's prescriptions: they provide contexts and practical elaborations that bring out the wider implications of his crptic expressions.
