## Comments and Research Notes

### Verse 1

Taittiriya Aranyaka (TA) 1.26.5.5,7 provides a particularly tight parallel to the Kural: आपो वै वृष्टिः, water is rain, and पर्जन्यो वर्षुको भवति, connecting that rain with Parjanya, the rain-bringing power of the heavens. This resonates with the kural's வானின் றுலகம் வழங்கி வருதலாற் றான்: since the rain that comes from the sky and sustains the world. TA 1.26.5.7 then states explicitly, अमृतं वा आपः, water indeed is amrita, directly corresponding to the kural's தானமிழ்தம். The following अमृतस्यानन्तरित्यै further emphasizes that water is the means of attaining or preserving the continuity of immortality, giving particular force to its designation as amrita. Finally, य एवं वेद, he who knows thus, closely resonates with என்றுணரற் பாற்று, the recognition that it is to be understood in this way. Thus both passages connect rain, the sustaining waters of skies, and amrita, presenting the rain that descends from the sky as the world's life-giving nectar.

### Verse 2

The Taittiriya Samhita verse 5.4.9.2 provides an excellent parallel for this Kural. It is immediately clear that अन्नमिव खलु वै वर्षम् (rain, indeed, is as it were food) is remarkably close to துப்பாயதூஉம் மழை (rain itself becomes food). The striking correspondence in rhythmic structure is hard to miss: अन्नस्यान्नस्य ... अन्नस्यान्नस्य ... अन्नमन्नम् ... वर्षम् repeatedly turns upon अन्न and then culminates in the single वर्षम्, just as the Kural repeatedly turns upon துப்பு and culminates in the single மழை.

The deeper semantic parallel becomes clearer once we extricate the relevant structure from the ritual machinery: अन्नस्यान्नस्यावरुद्ध्यै ... अवरुद्धं ह्यस्य अन्नम् \| अन्नमिव खलु वै वर्षम् \|. The later अवरुद्धम् connects back to the preceding अवरुद्ध्यै across the intervening ritual material. The ritual passage thus contains an underlying causal nexus: food is obtained for the oblation, food is offered as oblation (जुहोति), and the ritual nexus leads through rain to the securing of food and grain. Valluvar makes note of this ritual context a few verses later when he says that சிறப்பொடு பூசனை for the வானோர் can no longer proceed on earth if rain fails. The kural compresses a larger rain–ritual–food nexus into its economical two-part proposition: rain makes food for the food-eater, and rain itself becomes food.

The trichotomy of that-which-eats-food, that-which-becomes-food, and that-which-is-food in the kural's vocabulary of துப்பார், துப்பாயது, and துப்பு, has a striking analogue in the Vedic vocabulary of अन्नम्, अन्नाद्यम्, and अन्नादः. The Taittiriya tradition ultimately dissolves these distinctions into the unity of Brahman, allowing अन्नम् to encompass all three. This is expressed explicitly in Taittiriya Upanishad, Ananda-valli, 3.10.6, where the realized person declares: अहमन्नमहमन्नमहमन्नम् \| अहमन्नादोऽहमन्नादोऽहमन्नादः \|\| — “I am food ... I am the food-eater.” The passage therefore provides a particularly apt Sanskrit analogue for the Kural's ability to move between துப்பார், the eaters, and துப்பு, that which is eaten, while making the same semantic field encompass the thing that becomes food.

The अवरुद्ध्यै resonates with the ஆக்கி in துப்பாக்கி. This is corroborated by the Maitrayani Samhita, which explicitly connects वृष्टि, अन्नाद्य, and अवरुध्: in verse 2.4.8: वृष्टिर् वै देवेभ्योऽन्नाद्यम् अपाक्रामत् \| तेभ्यो वृष्टिम् अन्नाद्यम् अवारुन्द्ध \| ... वृष्ट्या अन्नाद्यस्यावरुद्ध्यै \|\| — “Rain, indeed, departed from the gods as that which becomes food; they secured rain as that which becomes food ... the rite is for securing that which becomes food through rain.” The verse provides independent Vedic corroboration of the துப்பாக்கி / துப்பாய movement in the Kural.

The trichotomy, and its tight association with rain, is also consistent with Kalingar's commentary on the kural: உண்பவர் யாவர்க்கும் உணவுப் பொருளாய் இன்னும் அவற்றை உளவாக உறுதிபண்ணிக் கொடுப்பதூஉஞ் செய்து; உண்பவர் யாவர்க்குந் தானே உணவுமாய் நிற்பதூஉம் மழை என்றவாறு. Kalingar first describes rain as providing and securing the food-substances for all who eat, and then explicitly says that rain itself stands as food for all who eat, matching my proposed reading of the kural and its parallel.

### Verse 3

The correspondence between the kural and Kalika Purana 20.113 is remarkably tight, with virtually every significant fragment of the kural finding a direct counterpart in the Sanskrit: விண்ணின்று பொய்ப்பின் (if the rain from heaven fails) corresponds to वृष्ट्यभावे तु (in the absence of rain), வியனுலகம் (the wide world) to लोकानाम् (of the people/world), உண்ணின்று (the condition of not having food to eat) to आहाराः क्षीणतां गताः (food supplies having dwindled), and உடற்றும் பசி (hunger that afflicts) to दुर्भिक्षव्यसनोपेते सर्वलोके (when the whole world is afflicted by the calamity of famine). 

The Devas further speak of the consequence in 20.128: अन्नवैकल्यमासाद्य म्रियन्ते बहवः प्रजाः, that is, many people perish upon encountering the lack of food. They add that, with the sacrificial shares diminished, भोग्यहीनास्तथा वयम्, they themselves are deprived of what is to be enjoyed. The same progression is corroborated by the Mahabharata, Shanti Parva 12.139, where the precedent of a twelve-year drought produces calamities extending far beyond the destruction of agriculture and trade: social order and spiritual practices collapse, robbery becomes widespread, people are driven even to cannibalism, and spiritual leaders themselves are reduced to consuming dog meat, despite its being strictly forbidden. Thus, all three texts portray failure of rain as the beginning of a cascading food crisis whose consequences ultimately extend to the disruption of the entire social, moral, and spiritual order.

### Verse 4

For the kural, I propose as a primary parallel Mahabharata, Shanti Parva 12.139.19 (BORI Critical Ed.). First, it is important to consider the duration and severity of the drought before concluding that farmers will abandon their livelihood and, in the Indic context, their agricultural duty. While even a single seasonal failure of rainfall could be devastating to individual farmers, a blanket statement about farmers as a group should be understood as describing an extreme situation in which other sources of irrigation have also become nonviable because of prolonged drought.

The context of the Shanti Parva 12.139 is therefore crucial to the parallel. Bhishma recounts an ancient catastrophic drought lasting twelve years, beginning at 12.139.13: अनावृष्टिरभूद्घोरा राजन्द्वादशवार्षिकी. In 12.139.16–18, he describes the disappearance of dew and clouds and the depletion of rivers, lakes, wells, and springs: नद्यः संक्षिप्ततोयौघाः क्वचिदन्तर्गताभवन्, followed by सरांसि सरितश्चैव कूपाः प्रस्रवणानि च.

The proposed parallel occurs in the very next verse, which states that agriculture has ceased, उत्सन्नकृषि, which directly corresponds to Valluvar’s ஏரி னுழாஅ ருழவர், farmers do not plough. The absence of rainfall, अनावृष्टि*, is implicit in the Mahabharata passage through the immediately preceding twelve-year drought narrative, whereas Valluvar expresses the cause poetically as புயலென்னும் வாரி வளங்குன்றிக் கால், when the rain-bearing cloud, the source of rain-water, has diminished in its abundance. Valluvar states the consequence in active terms, whereas the Mahabharata uses a compact passive construction and then proceeds to describe its wider ramifications: the cessation of cattle-rearing, markets and shops, guild and community meetings, and public festivals.

Given the strong parallels between the Kalika Purana and several other kurals in this chapter, it is also worth noting that, within the same related sequence, Markandeya records: पर्जन्याश्च ततो नष्टास्ततो वृष्टिर्नचाभवत् (KP 20.112). This closely parallels புயலென்னும் வாரி வளங்குன்றிக் கால். The following verse states: वृष्ट्यभावे तु लोकानामाहाराः क्षीणतां गताः 
(KP 20.113). The resulting scarcity of food implicitly signals the cessation of agriculture, corresponding to Valluvar’s more explicit statement that farmers cease ploughing: ஏரி னுழாஅ ருழவர். This convergence is not surprising given the close corroboration among the kural chapter, the Kalika Purana, and the Mahabharata’s account of a prolonged and catastrophic drought.

#### Alternative Agricultural Reading

I was initially tempted to read the kural as suggesting that the farmer, owing to his knowledge of the impending failure of the rains, will conserve his effort. Given the context above, I would not present these passages as parallels, but they are nevertheless interesting:

  - Mahabharata, Shanti Parva 12.137.75 (BORI Critical Edition): यस्तु वर्षमविज्ञाय क्षेत्रं कर्षति कर्षकः \| हीनः पुरुषकारेण सस्यं नैवाश्नुते ततः \|\|, suggests that a farmer who ploughs without understanding the rainfall will not obtain a proper harvest.
  - The same idea is emphasized in the agricultural treatise Krishi Parashara 10: वृष्टिमूला कृषिः सर्वा वृष्टिमूलं च जीवनम् \| तस्मादादौ प्रयत्नेन वृष्टिज्ञानं समाचरेत् \|\|; Agriculture and life have rainfall as their root, and therefore one should first make every effort to understand rainfall.
  - A modern Sanskrit composition, Alivilāsa 4.53, similarly states: अन्येषु हेतुषु तु सत्स्वपि कर्ष्टचेष्टा स्यान्निष्फला जलधरो यदि न प्रवर्षेत् \| साधारणो जलधरो हि तेन हेतुः सा रीतिरप्रतिहता परमेश्वरेऽपि \|\|, that is, the effort expended in ploughing is fruitless if the rain-bearing cloud does not rain, and then extends the metaphor even to the Supreme Lord.

All of these passages accord well with the spirit of the kural chapter, particularly its emphasis on the dependence of agriculture upon rainfall. Nevertheless, this is not my preferred reading of the essence of the kural, especially in light of the chapter’s context and the Puranic corroboration discussed above. The Mahabharata 12.139 parallel is better understood not as a farmer prudently declining to plough in anticipation of rain failure, but as the cessation of agriculture itself under conditions of prolonged and catastrophic drought.

### Verse 5

Taittiriya Aranyaka (TA) 1.3.11 presents the same rain-dependent duality as the kural: या वै प्रजा भ्रश्यन्ते \| संवथ्सरात् ता भ्रश्यन्ते — those people who decline, decline from the year — corresponding to கெடுப்பதூஉம்; while याः प्रतिष्ठन्ति \| संवत्सरे ताः प्रतिष्ठन्ति — those who prosper/become established, are established in the year — corresponds to எடுப்பதூஉம். The repeated संवत्सर makes the agricultural, annual cycle explicit: when the rains fail, or are otherwise adverse, the people decline, while favorable rains sustain their establishment. And the concluding वर्षाभ्य इत्यर्थः, “the meaning is with reference to the rains, identifies rain as the force behind both outcomes, exactly as the kural assigns both கெடுப்பதும் and எடுப்பதும் to மழை. The TA leaves கெட்டார்க்குச் சார்வாய் implicit: the juxtaposition of भ्रश्यन्ते (decline/fall away) and प्रतिष्ठन्ति (become established) itself suggests the rain-dependent restoration of those who have fallen.

In Parimelazhakar’s commentary of the kural, பூமியின்கண் வாழ்வாரை (those who live upon the earth) fits naturally with the agricultural orientation of the TA’s प्रजा within this rain-governed annual cycle. He then makes the same duality explicit: பூமியின்கண் வாழ்வாரைப் பெய்யாது நின்று கெடுப்பதூஉம்; அவ்வாறு கெட்டார்க்குத் துணையாய்ப் பெய்து முன் கெடுத்தாற் போல எடுப்பதூஉம்; இவை எல்லாம் வல்லது மழை. 'எல்லாம்' என்றது, அம்மக்கள் முயற்சி வேறுபாடுகளால் கெடுத்தல் எடுத்தல்கள் தாம் பலவாதல் நோக்கி. 'வல்லது' என்பது அவாய் நிலையான் வந்தது. மழையினது ஆற்றல் கூறியவாறு. His concluding மழையினது ஆற்றல் கூறியவாறு (thus the power of rain has been stated) provides a striking corroboration of the TA’s वर्षाभ्य इत्यर्थः: in both readings, rain is the agency capable of producing either ruin or restoration.

### Verse 6

For this kural, I propose Brihat Samhita 23.3 as the primary parallel. Valluvar is unlikely to have devoted an entire kural to the mundane observation that if not a drop of rain falls, even the tip of green grass will be hard to see. In connection with this parallel, I suggest that he is encoding a rainfall-monitoring tradition in which drops on the tips of grass serve as evidence of rainfall. The Brihat Samhita verse itself says that the amount of rainfall can first (see the postscript below) be assessed from the mark left on the earth or from drops remaining on the tips of grass, making बिन्दवस्तृणाग्रेषु part of an explicit rainfall-measurement procedure rather than merely a poetic image. The agricultural context is reinforced by the other kural in this chapter, ஏரி னுழாஅ ருழவர், and by the discussion elsewhere in this chapter of farmers learning the science of rainfall.

The uncommon construction बिन्दवस्तृणाग्रेषु has a remarkable resonance with the kural’s துளி, பசும்புல், and தலை; वृष्टिः corresponds directly to Valluvar’s more poetic விசும்பிற் றுளி வீழ்(தல்), drops falling from the sky. On this reading, துளி does double duty: it denotes the rain-drops that do not fall, and the drops consequently not seen on the grass or its tips. தலை, besides denoting நுனி (tip, point, end, apex, summit), can indicate இடம் (place or position), as in the Sangam text Purananuru 15, நனந்தலை நல்லெயில், glossed as அவருடைய அகலிய இடத்தை யுடைய நல்ல அரண்களை. This permits the economical possibility that the drops are understood both “on” the green grass and “at the tips” of the green grass, closely echoing तृणाग्रेषु. 

For those who prefer the mundane reading, which accords with the traditional commentarial interpretation, the following alternative parallels may be offered; Sanskrit literature is replete with descriptions of vegetation disappearing when the rains fail. In the Kalika Purana, which also parallels several kurals in this chapter, the Devas state at 20.126–127: न च दानादिधर्माश्च न तपांसि क्षितौ क्वचित् \| नैव वर्षति पर्जन्यः क्षीणतोयाभवत् क्षितिः \|\| क्षीणाः सर्वास्तथौषध्यः शस्या लोकाः समाकुलाः \| दस्युभिः पीडिता विप्रा वेदवादं न कुर्वते \|\|, explicitly connecting the absence of rain with the drying of the earth and the depletion of herbs and crops; the loss of green grass is implicit within this broader vegetation failure. In the Mahabharata, Vana Parva 3.186.59 (BORI Critical Edition), यच्च काष्ठं तृणं चापि शुष्कं चार्द्रं च भारत \| सर्वं तद्भस्मसाद्भूतं दृश्यते भरतर्षभ \|\| explicitly mentions तृणं (grass), with the loss of greenness implicit in its reduction to ash. The context is the prolonged drought introduced at 3.186.56, अनावृष्टि … बहुवार्षिकी, followed by the drying of the waters in 3.186.58: ततो दिनकरैर्दीप्तैः सप्तभिर्मनुजाधिप \| पीयते सलिलं सर्वं समुद्रेषु सरित्सु च \|\|.

Postscript: The wider procedure in Brihat 23 is worth noting. The initial rainfall is first assessed by the mark it leaves on the earth or by बिन्दवस्तृणाग्रेषु (23.3); the extent of the first shower is then used to predict the area of subsequent rainfall (23.4), while the asterisms under which the preliminary rain falls are used to predict the recurrence and failure of rain in the rainy season (23.5). Verses 23.6–9 go further, assigning specific quantities of subsequent rainfall in "dronas" according to the asterisms, with 23.10 adding celestial disturbances as qualifications. Thus the grass-tip observation is explicitly the first of a progressively more elaborate system of rainfall assessment and prediction.

### Verse 7

The Vayu Purana, Uttarardha 38.137 and 38.147 verses provide a close parallel to the kural. The narrative describes an unusual hundred-year drought: तदा भवत्यनावृष्टिस्तदा सा शतवार्षिकी, followed by the devastation of the earth, culminating in साद्रिनद्यर्णवा पृथ्वी विस्नेहा समपद्यत. The correspondence is especially close: अनावृष्टि corresponds to what Valluvar poetically expresses as தடிந்தெழிலி தானல்காதாகி விடின் (if the rain-clouds, having been destroyed, do not give), implying the absence of rain; अर्णवा corresponds directly to நெடுங்கடலுந், identifying the ocean;  विस्नेहा समपद्यत, became devoid of moisture, closely corresponds to தன்னீர்மை குன்றுந், the ocean losing its own essential moisture. This विस्नेहा समपद्यत has a remarkable resonance with தன்னீர்மை குன்றுந், which is a more nuanced expression than simply saying that the water is reduced or that the ocean dries up. The explicit mention of पृथ्वी also resonates with Manakkudavar’s gloss, நிலமேயன்றி நெடியகடலும் தனது தன்மை குறையும். Nor is there ambiguity about अर्णवा referring to the oceans: the same account explicitly uses महोदधिः and समुद्रेभ्यो in 38.154–155: द्वीपाश्च पर्वताश्चैव वर्षाण्यथ महोदधिः … and समुद्रेभ्यो नदीभ्यश्च पातालेभ्यश्च सर्वतः पिबन्नपः, describing the water being consumed from the oceans, rivers, and subterranean regions.

#### Additional Corroborations in the Brahma Purana and the Kalika Purana

The Brahma Purana 232.14–23 corroborates essentially the same episode and in strikingly similar terms. It opens with अनावृष्टिरतीवोग्रा जायते शतवार्षिकी, explicitly specifying the hundred-year drought, and then states that the waters of the समुद्रान् सरितः and other sources are exhausted: समुद्रान् सरितः शैलाञ् शैलप्रस्रवणानि च पातालेषु च यत् तोयं तत् सर्वं नयति क्षयम्. The climactic description, साद्रिनगार्णवाभोगं निःस्नेहमभिजायते, is particularly significant: the earth, with its mountains, cities, and oceans, becomes निःस्नेह, devoid of moisture, closely reinforcing the kural’s தன்னீர்மை குன்றும். 

Given the strong parallels in the Kalika Purana to several other kurals in this chapter, it is also worth noting that, within the same related sequence, Markandeya records जग्मुः क्षोभं परं देवाः सागराश्च ग्रहास्तथा (20.116): the क्षोभं … सागराश्च marks the disturbance of the oceans. This is not itself a parallel to the kural, but it corroborates the wider Puranic conception of prolonged failure of rain producing consequences that extend even to the oceans.

#### The 100-year drought and the 12-year drought

The timeline/duration of the drought is important for understanding the nuance of the kural. Valluvar does not specify a duration, but an occasional failure of rain for a single year, or even a drought lasting several years, would not ordinarily be expected to produce a perceptible change in the oceans; water bodies on land would show the effects much sooner. 

The Mahabharata, Shanti Parva 12.139, provides an illuminating narrative in which Bhishma recounts for Yudhishthira an ancient catastrophic drought lasting twelve years: अनावृष्टिरभूद्घोरा राजन्द्वादशवार्षिकी. In 12.139.16–18, he describes the disappearance of dew and clouds and the depletion of rivers, lakes, wells, and springs: नद्यः संक्षिप्ततोयौघाः क्वचिदन्तर्गताभवन्, followed by सरांसि सरितश्चैव कूपाः प्रस्रवणानि च. Yet the **oceans are notably absent** from this catalogue of waters that dry up. This stands in striking contrast to the Brahma and Vayu Purna accounts of a hundred-year drought, where the अर्णवा/ocean and the loss of स्नेह/moisture are explicitly brought into the devastation. Thus, while many of the other phenomena in the kural chapter, as well as in the corresponding Kālika Purana material, find resonance in the Mahabharata’s account of the twelve-year drought, the distinctive claim that நெடுங்கடலும் தன்னீர்மை குன்றும் finds its closest Puranic parallel in the extraordinary शतवार्षिकी अनावृष्टि narrative.

### Verse 8

The correspondence of Kalika Purana 20.112 (Khemaraj 1948 ed.) is strikingly close in both vocabulary and causal sequence to the kural. சிறப்பொடு பூசனை செல்லாது corresponds to यज्ञाभावात्, the cessation of yajna or sacrificial worship; வானம் வறக்குமேல் corresponds to पर्जन्याश्च ततो नष्टाः ततो वृष्टिर्न चाभवत्, the disappearance of the rain-clouds and consequent absence of rainfall; and வானோர்க்கு மீண்டு corresponds to देवानामन्नं सर्वं क्षयं गतम्, the destruction of the devas’ sustenance.

The narrative context makes the parallel still more significant. Following the curse on Chandra, Markandeya explains in 20.110-111: ततस्तेनाभिभूतः स यक्ष्मणा रोहिणीपतिः \| क्षयं जगामानुदिनं ग्रीष्मे क्षुद्रा नदी यथा \|\| अथ चन्द्रे क्षीयमाणे सर्वौषध्यो गताः क्षयम् \| क्षयं यातास्वौषधिषु न यज्ञः समवर्तत \|\|. As Chandra wanes, and due to the heat everyday the rivers dy up, and then the plants and crops perish, and with their destruction yajna can no longer proceed. The devas, along with Markandeya, then approach Brahma to deliberate over the calamity, and this causal sequence is repeated thrice in Chapter 20. The devas state in 20.125: न क्वचित् सम्प्रवर्तन्ते यज्ञा लोके पितामह \| निराधारा निरातंकाः प्रजाः सर्वां क्षयं गताः \|\|; Brahma subsequently confirms in 20.154: औषध्यभावाल्लोकेऽस्मिन् न यज्ञः सम्प्रवर्तते \| यज्ञाभावादनावृष्टिस्ततः सर्वप्रजाक्षयः \|\|. The Puranic narrative therefore makes explicit a vicious cycle that Valluvar compresses into the rain chapter: crop failure → cessation of yajna/பூசனை → failure of rain → destruction of the material, social, and spiritual foundations necessary to sustain yajna.

Kalingar’s commentary is also noteworthy because it expands சிறப்பொடு பூசனை செல்லாது beyond a narrowly sacrificial sense: தேவர் மாட்டும் பிதிர்கள் மாட்டும் விசேஷித்துச் செய்யும் கருமங்களும், இங்ஙனம் நிமித்தீகரித்துச் செய்தலின்றி உயர்ந்தோர் நாடோறும் செய்து வருகின்ற நற்கருமங்களும் மற்று இவை இரண்டும் சென்று தலைப்படாது-—both the special rites performed for the gods and ancestors and the virtuous daily observances of the great do not proceed. He then glosses வானம் வறக்குமேல் வானோர்க்கும் ஈண்டு simply as மழையானது வறந்து போமாயின் தேவர்கட்கும் இவ்விடத்தில், that is, if the rain dries up, then for the gods also here have deprivation. This is significant for the proposed parallel because Kalingar’s interpretation makes பூசனை encompass not merely particular offerings to the gods and ancestors but the wider sphere of religious and virtuous observance. The Kalika Purana likewise presents the failure of औषधि and food, and hence of यज्ञ, as the beginning of a much wider civilizational collapse: with the failure of rain, food supplies are exhausted, famine prevails, charity and dharma cease, the people become degraded and driven to greed and wrongdoing, social disorder and violence spread, the crops and medicinal plants perish, Vedic practice declines, many people die from want of food, and even the gods become deprived of their sacrificial shares. Thus the Kural’s terse பூசனை செல்லாது anticipates not simply the cessation of ritual, but the cascading destruction of the material and moral conditions that sustain religious life.

A further parallel appears in the Mahabharata, Shanti Parva 12.139, where Bhishma recalls for Yudhishhira an ancient precedent from the junction of the Treta and Dvapara ages. A catastrophic twelve-year drought brings widespread devastation, scattering people through hunger and fear and bringing ordinary social and ritual life to a halt. The drought is explicitly described in 12.139.13 (BORI Critical ed.): त्रेताद्वापरयोः संधौ पुरा दैवविधिक्रमात् \| अनावृष्टिरभूद्घोरा राजन्द्वादशवार्षिकी \|\|. A subsequent verse gives an especially close parallel to the present argument in 12.139.18: उपशुष्कजलस्थाया विनिवृत्तसभाप्रपा \| निवृत्तयज्ञस्वाध्याया निर्वषट्कारमङ्गला \|\|. Here the drying up of water sources is accompanied by the cessation of yajña and svādhyāya, and by the disappearance of the ritual acts and observances associated with the vaṣaṭ offering. Like the Kālikā Purāṇa, therefore, the Mahabharata presents drought as destroying not merely agricultural productivity but the ritual and religious practices sustained by the availability of food and water—providing a striking contextual parallel to Valluvar’s சிறப்பொடு பூசனை செல்லாது வானம் வறக்குமேல்.

### Verse 9

The correspondence of Kalika Puarana 20.126 and the kural is striking: வானம் வழங்கா தெனின் (if the heavens withhold their bounty) finds its counterpart in नैव वर्षति पर्जन्यः, while தானந் தவமிரண்டும் தங்கா is echoed almost verbatim in न च दानादिधर्माश्च न तपांसि. Markandeya had already corroborated the same principle in Kalika Purana 20.114, declaring दानधर्मादिकं किञ्चिन्न लोकस्य प्रवर्तते, that charitable and other meritorious practices no longer function in society when all people become devoid of sattva and their faculties are overwhelmed by greed (लोभेनोपहतेन्द्रियाः). The same message finds a broader narrative parallel in the Mahabharata, Shanti Parva chapter 12.139, where the precedent of a twelve-year drought illustrates how prolonged deprivation undermines the conditions sustaining religious observances and social virtue.

### Verse 10

For this kural. I propose Kalika Purana 20.113-115 (Khemaraj 1948 ed.) as the primary parallel. The kural’s வானின் றமையா தொழுக்கு is expressed here with unusual specificity: when rain fails, वृष्ट्यभावे, the people become सत्त्वहीनाः, their inner moral quality or disciplined disposition lost, their senses overcome by greed (लोभेनोपहतेन्द्रियाः), and they consequently engage in पाप and कुकर्म. सत्त्वहीनाः is especially resonant with the loss of ஒழுக்கு: it describes not merely weakness or deprivation, but the loss of the inner quality that sustains proper conduct, while पापमेव तदा चक्रुः कुकर्मरतयश्च makes the resulting breakdown of conduct explicit. The collapse of social and spiritual discipline is further corroborated by the Devas in KP 20.127: क्षीणाः सर्वास्तथैौषध्यः शस्या लोकाः समाकुलाः \| दस्युभिः पीडिता विप्रा वेदवादं न कुर्वते \|\|, where even the Brahmanas, troubled by robbers, cease their Vedic discourse.

The kural’s நீரின் றமையா துலகு is likewise compressed into the drought landscape: लोकानामाहाराः directly shows the world deprived of food when rain fails, while the surrounding narrative depicts the progressive failure of the material conditions on which the world depends. The twelve-year drought of the Mahabharata, Shanti Parva 12.139, makes this same compression especially vivid. Bhishma catalogs the consequences in sequence: उपशुष्कजलस्थाया विनिवृत्तसभाप्रपा \| निवृत्तयज्ञस्वाध्याया निर्वषट्कारमङ्गला \|\|, with water sources drying up, public water stations falling into disuse, and yajna, svadhyaya, and vashatkara ceasing; the following verse adds उत्सन्नकृषिगोरक्ष्या निवृत्तविपणापणा \| निवृत्तपूगसमया संप्रनष्टमहोत्सवा \|\|, the collapse of agriculture and cattle-keeping, commerce, communal institutions, and festivals. Valluvar compresses this entire catalogue of material, social, and spiritual breakdown into the single word ஒழுக்கு: when water fails, not only does the world cease to function, but the order of conduct that makes it a functioning world fails with it.


### Chapter Summary

My reading of this chapter in connection with the Vedas and Puranas brings out a coherent progression in Valluvar's treatment of rain. Placing the kurals in Manakkudavar's order makes this structure especially visible. The chapter moves from the dependence of ritual and religious observance upon rain, through the dependence of food, agriculture, and social life upon rainfall, and finally to the deeper claim that rain sustains the order of life itself.

The Kalika Purana provides the most substantial continuous Puranic parallel to the first seven kurals in Manakkudavar's order. The relevant material occurs principally in KP 20.111–116 and 20.125–128. Taken as a block, these passages trace the same interconnected consequences of rainfall found across Valluvar's opening seven statements: rain sustains the sacrificial order and the gods; its absence brings the failure of ritual and religious observance; the failure of rain leads to the loss of food and the cessation of charity and austerity; prolonged drought destroys agriculture and produces famine; and the resulting deprivation extends from the material world into social and moral life.

The Vedic parallels provide Shruti pramana for Valluvar's central assertions in the last three kurals of the chapter in Manakkudavar's order. Taittiriya Aranyaka 1.26.5,7 declares **अमृतं वा आपः** and **आपो वै वृष्टिः**—water is amrita; water is rain. The Taittiriya Samhita 5.4.9.2 **अन्नमिव खलु वै वर्षम्** is especially striking, with the rest of the mantra echoing the celebrated polyptoton **துப்பார்க்குத் துப்பாய துப்பாக்கித் துப்பார்க்குத் துப்பாய தூஉம் மழை** through its repeated **अन्नम् ... अन्नम् ... वर्षम्** structure, paralleling the kural's **துப்பு ... துப்பு ... மழை**. Taittiriya Aranyaka 1.3.11 declares **याः प्रजाः भ्रश्यन्ते ... याः प्रतिष्ठन्ते ... वर्षाभ्यः**—rain is associated with both decline and establishment.

The agricultural and meteorological kurals also find distinctive parallels. For ஏரி னுழாஅ ருழவர், the twelve-year drought of the Mahabharata Shanti Parva 12.139 provides the strongest contextual parallel: after prolonged failure of rainfall and the depletion of water sources, agriculture itself ceases, while the Kalika Purana's adjacent account of failed rain and food scarcity corroborates this agricultural consequence. I also presented an alternative agricultural reading of this kural, drawing on the Krishi Parashara and Mahabharata injunctions that the farmer must learn the science of rainfall. This dovetails nicely with **பசும்புற் றலைகாண் பரிது**, for which Brihat Samhita 23.3 offers a more specialized reading in which drops upon the tips of grass form part, suggesting a possible meteorological procedure for assessing rainfall. This offers an alternative background to Valluvar's otherwise cryptic image of raindrops and green grass, although the traditional mundane interpretation remains possible. It is plausible that Valluvar was encoding multiple messages in these cryptic kural aphorisms; and even if some of these connections are retrospective, they remain fascinating in the context of the wider Indic literature on rain.

The **நெடுங்கடலுந் தன்னீர்மை குன்றுந்** is especially well matched by the extraordinary hundred-year drought narratives of the Vayu and Brahma Puranas, which corroborate each other. These accounts explicitly describe the depletion of the oceans and the earth becoming devoid of moisture, providing a close parallel to Valluvar's striking claim that even the great ocean loses its own essential moisture when the rain-bearing clouds fail. The contrast with the Mahabharata's twelve-year drought is instructive: the latter describes the drying of terrestrial water sources, while the hundred-year drought narratives extend the devastation to the oceans themselves.  While Valluvar does not mention any timescale, it is also common sense that social collapse and the drying of the land can occur within a few years of the failure of rains, whereas a much more prolonged drought would be needed for the oceans to noticeably dry up. The specific structure, sequence, and manner in which the impact of prolonged drought is presented by Valluvar have remarkable lexical and semantic parallels in the tight sequence of verses in the Kalika Purana. Much of this progression is independently corroborated in the Mahabharata's memory of the twelve-year drought, if in more graphic and gory detail. 

Taken together, these parallels suggest that Valluvar's rain chapter is not merely a collection of observations about rainfall. Valluvar's high-level spiritual statements find Shruti pramana in the Taittiriya Yajurveda, in remarkably close terms, while the Puranas preserve a vivid Indic civilizational memory of the consequences of rain and prolonged drought. The shared connection between rainfall, agriculture, wellbeing, social order, and spiritual practices across the kural and its parallels is clear; the vocabulary and message match consistently.
