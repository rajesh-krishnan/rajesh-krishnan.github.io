## Comments and Research Notes

### Verse 1

I propose Kamandakiya Nitisara XIV.94, read together with XIV.65, as the primary parallel for this kural. Kamandaka first identifies the vulnerabilities affecting the seven constituents of the state, including those of the fort in XIV.65, and then prescribes their removal. The correspondence is particularly close between அறிவற்றங் காக்குங் கருவி and बुद्ध्या व्यसनस्य व्यवरोपणम्: intelligence (बुद्ध्या) is the means by which vulnerabilities (व्यसनस्य) are eliminated (व्यवरोपणम्). The kural's கருவி need not have a separately expressed Sanskrit equivalent, since the instrumental बुद्ध्या itself conveys the means; likewise, காத்தல் is understood here in the sense of warding off or removing a vulnerability. 

Kamandaka's preceding verse XIV.65 identifies that the deterioration of defensive machinery, ramparts and moats, the absence of weapons, and the depletion of grass, fuel and provisions constitute the vulnerabilities of a fort (दुर्गव्यसनम्). Read alongside XIV.94, the two verses establish the full correspondence: XIV.65 identifies what compromises the fort, while XIV.94 prescribes the application of intelligence and strength to eliminate such defects without delay. செறுவார்க்கு முள்ளழிக்கலாகா வரண் is thus the outcome of this intelligent removal of defects: a fortification that enemies cannot penetrate to destroy what lies within.

Kamandakiya Nitisara XV.69 further corroborates this reading: अरिगणा नियतं व्यसने स्थितान् परिभवन्ति भवन्ति च दुश्छिदः \| अपगतव्यसनास् तु बुधा रिपून् परिभवन्ति भवन्ति च दुश्छिदः \|\|. Enemies invariably overcome those who remain afflicted by vulnerabilities, who consequently become easy to assail; the wise, however, having freed themselves from such vulnerabilities, overcome their enemies and become difficult to assail. The correspondence is especially suggestive in अपगतव्यसनास् तु बुधाः, where बुधाः evokes அறிவு, while अपगतव्यसनाः conveys the elimination of அற்றம்; the resulting दुश्छिदः, being difficult to assail, resonates with உள்ளழிக்கலாகா. This verse makes explicit the relationship implicit in the kural: freedom from vulnerabilities, achieved through intelligence, is what makes one difficult for enemies to overcome.

Kalingar's gloss is particularly valuable in elucidating the wider semantic range of அறிவு and அரண்: அரசர்க்கு ஆனை, குதிரை தேர் காலாள் என்னும் நாற்பெரும் சிறையும் அரணன்று. தமக்கு இங்ஙனம் சிறந்த அறிவு யாது? அதுவே தமக்கோர் இடையூறு வரும் வழி பாதுகாக்கும் படையாவது; அதுவுமன்றிப் பின்னும் பகைவேந்தர்க்கும் உள்புக்கு அழித்தல் அரிதாய அரணாவதும் அதுவே என்றவாறு. By asking சிறந்த அறிவு யாது? and answering that it is itself the படை that protects against impending obstacles and the அரண் that enemies find difficult to penetrate, Kalingar moves beyond the conventional understanding of அரண் to extend beyond foritfications and even the fourfold army of elephants, cavalry, chariots and infantry. His interpretation brings out precisely the conceptual movement in the kural: அறிவு is not simply an abstract faculty, but its application in anticipating and addressing vulnerabilities, thereby providing protection against threats. 

Kautilya's Arthashastra 2.3.1 provides a a complementary perspective on the practical realization of fortification: चतुर्दिशं जनपदान्ते साम्परायिकं दैवकृतं दुर्गं कारयेत् \| अन्तर्द्वीपं स्थलम् वा निम्नावरुद्धम् औदकम् \| प्रास्तरं गुहां वा पार्वतम् \| निरुदकस्तम्बम् इरिणं वा धान्वनम् \| खञ्जनोदकं स्तम्बगहनं वा वनदुर्गम् \|\|. The injunction दुर्गं कारयेत् prescribes the deliberate construction of fortifications, and Kautilya proceeds to prescribe their appropriate locations and types, followed by detailed prescriptions for their defensive arrangements. Read within the Arthashastra's larger framework of intelligent statecraft, this passage illustrates how the security of the kingdom must be translated into carefully conceived and executed defensive measures. It complements the kural's emphasis on intelligence as the instrument of protection, while Kamandaka XIV.94 makes the connection between intelligence and the removal of vulnerabilities more explicit.

Manusmriti 7.73 offers a more direct parallel to the defensive outcome: यथा दुर्गाश्रितानेतान्नोपहिंसन्ति शत्रवः \| तथाऽरयो न हिंसन्ति नृपं दुर्गसमाश्रितम् \|\|. Just as enemies cannot harm those sheltered within fortifications, so too cannot they harm a king who has taken refuge in a fort. This resonates with செறுவார்க்கு முள்ளழிக்கலாகா வரண், but remains a conventional affirmation of the protective function of fortifications; it does not convey the kural's more distinctive conception of intelligence as the instrument of protection.

Manusmriti 7.104–105 offers a particularly close corroboration of the Kural's conception of அறிவு as the instrument for guarding against அற்றம்: अमाययैव वर्तेत न कथञ्चन मायया \| बुध्येतारिप्रयुक्तां च मायां नित्यं स्वसंवृतः \|\| नास्य छिद्रं परो विद्याच्छिद्रं विद्यात्परस्य तु \| गूहेत्कूर्म इवाङ्गानि रक्षेद्विवरमात्मनः \|\|. The king should act without deceit, while remaining constantly alert to the stratagems employed by his enemies. His adversary should not discover his own vulnerabilities, whereas he should discern those of the adversary; like a tortoise withdrawing its limbs, he should conceal his own openings. The correspondence is particularly close between அற்றம் காக்கும் and नास्य छिद्रं परो विद्याच्छिद्रं विद्यात्परस्य तु; அற்றம், in the sense of மறைக்கத்தக்கது (that which must be concealed), corresponds to छिद्र, the vulnerability or opening that must not be discovered by the enemy. The injunction बुध्येत् (discern, recognize) further resonates with அறிவு, emphasizing the intelligent discernment of vulnerabilities. Manu, however, takes the principle further: whereas Valluvar focuses on guarding against one's own vulnerabilities, Manu makes this a reciprocal exercise of intelligence—concealing one's own छिद्र while discovering those of the adversary.

The correspondence is especially striking between அற்றம் காக்கும் and नास्य छिद्रं परो विद्याच्छिद्रं विद्यात्परस्य तु: அற்றம், in the sense of மறைக்கத்தக்கது (that which must be concealed), corresponds to छिद्र, the vulnerability or opening that must not be discovered by the enemy. The injunction बुध्येत् (discern, recognize) further resonates with அறிவு: the king must intelligently discern the stratagems of his adversary, discover the latter's vulnerabilities, and conceal his own. The passage thus brings out both dimensions of the Kural's formulation—intelligence as the means of discerning vulnerabilities and protection as their concealment from the adversary.


### Verse 2

I propose Muktika Upanishad 2.5–6 as the primary parallel for this kural. The correspondence is particularly close between சென்ற விடத்தாற் செலவிடா and प्राक्तनस्तदसौ यत्नाज्जेतव्यो: an existing undesirable tendency must be checked and overcome through deliberate effort, rather than allowed to continue unchecked. The expression தீதொரீஇ நன்றின்பா லுய்ப்பது corresponds to अशुभेषु समाविष्टं शुभेष्वेवावतारयेत्, bringing that which has become absorbed in the undesirable into the good. The Upanishad elaborates the latter movement through the image of the stream of mental dispositions (वासनासरित्), which flows along the paths of good and evil and must be directed towards the good through purposeful effort. The correspondence is therefore conceptual and closely aligned, although the Upanishad explicitly situates the principle in the governance of mental tendencies, whereas Valluvar leaves its subject and domain unstated. The additional parallels below corroborate the same principle in other contexts, including friendship, childrearing, and the administration of the state.

Yoga Vasishta 2.9.30–31 preserves an almost identical version of the primary parallel, with minor textual and metrical variations: शुभाशुभाभ्यां मार्गाभ्यां वहन्ती वासनासरित् \| पौरुषेण प्रयत्नेन योजनीया शुभे पथि \|\| अशुभेषु समाविष्टं शुभेष्वेवावतारयेत् \| स्वमनः पुरुषार्थेन बलेन बलिनां वर \|\|. The Yoga Vasishtha verse makes explicit what the Muktika passage leaves implicit in the object being redirected: स्वमनः, one's own mind, must be brought from its absorption in the undesirable into the good through purposeful effort and strength. 

Bhartrihari Niti Shatakam 73 applies the same corrective principle to the conduct of a true friend: पापान्निवारयति योजयते हिताय गुह्यं निगूहति गुणान् प्रकटीकरोति \| आपद्गतं च न जहाति ददाति काले सन्मित्रलक्षणमिदं निगदन्ति सन्तः \|\|. A true friend prevents one from wrongdoing and directs one towards what is beneficial; conceals one's secrets and makes one's virtues known; does not abandon one in adversity, and provides assistance at the appropriate time. The correspondence is particularly close between தீதொரீஇ நன்றின்பா லுய்ப்பது and पापान्निवारयति योजयते हिताय: preventing wrongdoing and directing another towards what is beneficial. Bhartrihari presents this corrective and beneficial guidance as a defining characteristic of friendship, सन्मित्रलक्षणमिदं निगदन्ति सन्तः, the wisdom of which is affirmed by the wise.

Muktika Upanishad 2.7 extends the principle to the guidance of a childlike mind: अशुभाच्चालितं याति शुभं तस्मादपीतरत् \| पौरुषेण प्रयत्नेन लालयेच्चित्तबालकम् \|\|. The childlike mind (चित्तबालकम्) is to be nurtured and guided through deliberate human effort, so that it moves away from the undesirable towards the good. Although the child is metaphorical here, likening the mind to a child that wanders hither and thither, the passage illuminates a further application of Valluvar's general principle to childrearing. In this reading, the injunction would be to restrain a wayward child from pursuing an undesirable course, prevent harmful outcomes, and guide the child towards a beneficial future. The Sanskrit passage thus supplies a pedagogical application without requiring that this be the exclusive subject of the kural.

Kamandakiya Nitisara XIV.18–19 provides an administrative and statecraft application through its treatment of calamities affecting the constituent elements of the state (प्रकृति-व्यसन): प्रकृतिव्यसनं यत् स्यात् तत् प्रशाम्य समुत्पतेत् \| अनयापनयाभ्यां तज् जायते दैवतोऽपि च \|\| यस्मात् तद् व्यस्यति श्रेयस् तस्माद् व्यसनम् उच्यते \| व्यसन्य् अधोऽधो व्रजति तस्मात् तत् परिवर्जयेत् \|\|. Kamandaka explains that such calamities may arise through faulty policy or administration (अनय), through adverse circumstances, or through forces beyond human control. They are termed व्यसन because they impair श्रेयस्, the welfare and prosperity of the state; left unchecked, they lead to progressively worsening decline and must therefore be averted. This provides a useful administrative application of the kural: when a constituent of the state or an undertaking is found to be on a failing course, the deterioration must be arrested and corrective measures applied to restore it to a beneficial condition. The correspondence lies in the underlying movement from an undesirable course towards a beneficial one: தீதொரீஇ corresponds to averting or removing व्यसन, while நன்றின்பா லுய்ப்பது corresponds to preserving and restoring श्रेयस्. Kamandaka thus brings the principle into the domain of public administration, where discernment must lead to timely remedial intervention.

#### Summary of the different readings 

The Muktika Upanishad's inward and spiritual interpretation is consistent with the traditional Tamil commentaries, which likewise understand the kural in terms of governing the mind and its movements. Manakkudavar explains உள்ளஞ் சென்ற விடத்தே உடம்பையுஞ் செல்லவிடாது, emphasizing that the body must not be allowed to follow wherever the mind goes; Parimelazhagar, in turn, interprets சென்ற விடத்தாற் செலவிடா as மனத்தை அதுசென்ற புலத்தின்கண் செல்ல விடாது, emphasizing that the mind must not be allowed to follow wherever the senses have gone. While the two commentators differ in the immediate object of restraint—bodily action for Manakkudavar and the mind's movement towards sensory objects for Parimelazhagar—both locate the principle in the intelligent governance of the mind and its impulses. This remains the primary reading.

A distinctive feature of this kural is that Valluvar does not specify what wanders, where it wanders, what causes the wandering, or who undertakes its correction. Nor does he define what constitutes the தீது to be avoided or the நன்று towards which it must be directed. The subject may be the mind, the senses, a person's conduct, a child, a friend, an administrative undertaking, or a constituent of the state. The undesirable course may arise from sensory distractions, wrongful conduct, unwholesome tendencies, defective judgment, poor instruction, faulty administration, or adverse circumstances. Correspondingly, the means of correction may be self-discipline, discrimination, advice, instruction, supervision, or remedial intervention; the desired good may be spiritual well-being, ethical conduct, the welfare of another, sound upbringing, or the prosperity of an undertaking or the state. The subject, agency, cause, and objective are all left open. The operative principle remains constant: recognize an undesirable course, prevent its continuation, and direct it towards a beneficial one.

I offer these additional interpretations as applications admitted by the kural, rather than as alternatives to its primary reading. Bhartrihari applies the corrective principle to the role of a true friend; the image of चित्तबालकम् in Muktika 2.7 invites a pedagogical application to childrearing; and Kamandaka's treatment of प्रकृतिव्यसन illustrates its application to the prevention and remediation of decline in the constituent elements of the state. These are not competing interpretations but different applications made possible by Valluvar's deliberately unqualified formulation. Although the traditional commentaries privilege the inward and spiritual reading, the kural's location within அரசியல் in பொருட்பால் makes its wider application to the intelligent direction of conduct and the administration of public affairs especially significant.


### Verse 3

I propose Yoga Vasishtha 2.18.3 as the primary parallel for this kural. The expression வாய்க் கேட்பினும் corresponds to वचनम्, the speech or statement that is heard, while யார்யார் is metaphorically illustrated through the contrasting extremes of बालकादपि (even from a child) and पद्मजन्मना (even when spoken by Brahmā), spanning the range from the least to the highest authority. The expression மெய்ப்பொருள் காண்பது அறிவு corresponds to युक्तियुक्तमुपादेयम्: wisdom consists in discerning what is supported by reason and therefore worthy of acceptance, irrespective of the speaker. The converse, अन्यत्तृणमिव त्याज्यमप्युक्तं पद्मजन्मना, makes the principle equally emphatic: what is unsound is to be rejected like straw even when spoken by Brahmā himself. The correspondence is close in its insistence that the worth of speech must be judged by its substance rather than the identity or standing of its source. One distinction remains: the Yoga Vasishtha explicitly prescribes acceptance or rejection after examination, whereas the kural expresses the discernment of மெய்ப்பொருள் without specifying the disposition to be made of what is heard.

The preceding verse, Yoga Vasishtha 2.18.2, further corroborates this principle at the level of authoritative texts: अपि पौरुषमादेयं शास्त्रं चेद्युक्तिबोधकम् \| अन्यत्त्वार्षमपि त्याज्यं भाव्यं न्याय्यैकसेविना \|\| — even a work composed by an ordinary human is to be accepted if it imparts understanding through reason, whereas one originating from a Rishi is to be rejected if it does not, by one devoted exclusively to what is reasonable. While 2.18.3 applies this principle to spoken words, 2.18.2 extends it to शास्त्र itself, denying automatic authority even to works of Rishis. he expression न्याय्यैकसेविना, denoting one devoted to what is reasonable, reinforces the kural’s மெய்ப்பொருள் காண்பது அறிவு: wisdom lies in discerning the truth through reasoned examination, rather than deferring to the authority of the speaker or source.

The traditional commentaries reinforce this reading of the kural and its correspondence with Yoga Vasishtha 2.18.3. Manakkudavar explains: “யாதொரு பொருளை யாவர் சிலர் சொல்லக் கேட்பினும் அப்பொருளினது உண்மையை யாராய்வது அறிவாவது. இது யாவர் சிலர் நட்டோராயினும் பகைவராயினும் அவர் கூறக் கேட்டவற்றில் தெள்ளியராய் ஆராய்ந்து துணித லறிவென்றது.” He thus emphasizes careful inquiry into the truth of what is heard, even when it comes from friends or enemies. 

Parimelazhagar develops the point further: “யாதொரு பொருளை யாவர் யாவர் சொல்லக் கேட்பினும்; அப்பொருளின் மெய்யாய பயனைக் காணவல்லது அறிவு. குணங்கள் மூன்றும் மாறி மாறி வருதல் யாவர்க்கும் உண்மையின், உயர்ந்த பொருள் இழிந்தார் வாயினும், இழிந்த பொருள் உயர்ந்தார் வாயினும், உறுதிப்பொருள் பகைவர்வாயினும், கெடுபொருள் நட்டார்வாயினும், ஒரோவழிக் கேட்கப்படுதலான், 'எப்பொருள் யார் யார் வாய்க் கேட்பினும்' என்றார். அடுக்கு, பன்மைபற்றி வந்தது. 'வாய்' என்பது அவர் அப்பொருளின்கண் பயிலாமை உணர்த்திநின்றது. மெய்யாதல், நிலைபெறுதல். சொல்வாரது இயல்பு நோக்காது, அப்பொருளின் பயன் நோக்கிக் கொள்ளுதல் ஒழிதல் செய்வது அறிவு என்பதாம்.”  His explanation makes explicit that qualities and their opposites occur in different people: elevated ideas may be expressed by those of low standing, inferior ideas by those of high standing, reliable truths by enemies, and harmful propositions by friends. The repeated and plural expressions யாவர் யாவர் and எப்பொருள் emphasize the unrestricted scope of the principle, while வாய் indicates that the speaker may not ordinarily be familiar with, or associated with, the subject being discussed. His concluding formulation is particularly apt: wisdom consists in setting aside the nature of the speaker and attending instead to the value of the substance communicated. This accords closely with the Yoga Vasishtha's insistence that युक्तियुक्तम् be accepted even from a child, and unsound speech rejected even when spoken by Brahmā.

Comprehensive as this explanation is, it does not exhaust the possible range of the kural. Its two expressions allow us to examine not only the identity and standing of the speaker and the substance of what is communicated, but also the circumstances, manner, and intended meaning of speech, together with the different intellectual operations through which its truth is discerned.

#### Expanded interpretive framework

Beyond the primary parallel, the expressions in the kural permit the following broader examination:

  - **யார்யார்வாய் — the speaker**
    - **Status:** The speaker may be of high, intermediate, or low standing, whether social, economic, political, administrative, educational, or religious. Neither authority nor lack of status should determine the credibility of the communication.
    - **Relationship:** The speaker may be a guru, friend, relative, adviser, trusted associate, or loyal employee; a hostile enemy or deceitful actor; or a neutral or unfamiliar person, such as an acquaintance, stranger, or member of the public. Each relationship presents its own possibility of partiality, hostility, or concealed intention.
  - **எப்பொருள் — what is communicated**
    - **Nature:** The communication may be pleasant, unpleasant, or neutral.
    - **Form:** It may be formal or informal, well-articulated or coarse, direct or indirect.
    - **Substance:** It may concern spiritual or philosophical teaching, economic matters, personal relationships, science, geopolitics, intelligence, or any other subject.
  - **மெய்ப்பொருள் காண்பது அறிவு — the discernment exercised**
    1. Spiritual and philosophical discernment.
    2. Deciphering indirect communication.
    3. Psychological discernment.
    4. Impartial extraction of beneficial truth.
    5. Verification of reports and allegations.

The following corroborating texts are organized around these five dimensions of மெய்ப்பொருள் காணுதல். Each necessarily involves consideration of both யார்யார்வாய் and எப்பொருள்: the speaker's identity and relationship, the nature and form of the speech, and its subject matter all affect the circumstances in which discernment is exercised.

##### 1. Spiritual and philosophical discernment

One dimension of மெய்ப்பொருள் காண்பது அறிவு is spiritual and philosophical discernment: penetrating beyond the apparent or literal meaning of a teaching to establish its deeper truth. In this application, யார்யார் may be the guru, learned teacher, the Shruti, or another philosophical authority; எப்பொருள் is the spiritual or philosophical teaching communicated; and மெய்ப்பொருள் காண்பது அறிவு is the inquiry through which its deeper truth is independently established. Yoga Vasishtha 2.18.6 describes this process: श्रुतायां प्राज्ञवदनाद्बुद्ध्वान्तं स्वयमेव च \| शनैःशनैर्विचारेण बुद्धौ संस्कार आगते \|\| — having heard from the wise and understood the conclusion oneself, the mind is progressively cultivated through inquiry. The sequence श्रुतायाम्, बुद्ध्वा स्वयमेव and शनैःशनैर्विचारेण illuminates the movement from hearing, through independent understanding, to reflective assimilation.

Vivekacudanmani 13 offers a further corroboration: भ्रान्त्यो अर्थस्य निश्चयो दृष्टो विचारेण हितोक्तितः \| न स्नानेन न दानेन प्राणायामशतेन वा \|\| — certainty regarding the truth is attained through inquiry into beneficial instruction, not through ritual bathing, giving, or hundreds of breath-control practices. Here, हितोक्तितः points to the teaching received from யார்யார், अर्थस्य identifies the truth or meaning to be discerned in எப்பொருள், and विचारेण expresses the means by which மெய்ப்பொருள் காண்பது அறிவு is exercised, culminating in अर्थस्य निश्चयः. 

##### 2. Deciphering indirect communication

A second dimension of மெய்ப்பொருள் காண்பது அறிவு is the ability to decipher indirect communication and recover the intended meaning concealed beneath its surface expression. The Mahabharata Adi Parva, in its account of the Jatugriha (lac house) episode, describes Duryodhana's plot to destroy the Pandavas at Varanavata and Vidura's attempt to warn Yudhishthira without alerting those around them. The episode is summarized in 1.2.103 (Gita Press edition): हितोपदेशश्च पथि धर्मराजस्य धीमतः \| विदुरेण कृतो यत्र हितार्थं म्लेच्छभाषया \|\| — Vidura, seeking the welfare of the wise Dharmaraja, conveys beneficial counsel to him on the journey in the Mleccha language. In this instance, யார்யார் is represented by Vidura, the trusted adviser and well-wisher; எப்பொருள் by the coded warning conveyed through Mleccha speech; and மெய்ப்பொருள் காண்பது அறிவு by Yudhishthira's discernment of the warning's intended meaning beyond its outward expression. 

##### 3. Psychological discernment

A third dimension of மெய்ப்பொருள் காண்பது அறிவு is psychological discernment: the ability to infer unexpressed intentions and emotional states from speech and outward behavior. Panchatantra 1.44–45 describes this distinction: उदीरितोऽर्थः पशुनापि गृह्यते हयाश्च नागाश्च वहन्ति चोदिताः \| अनुक्तमूहति पण्डितो जनः परेङ्गितज्ञानफला हि बुद्धयः \|\| आकारैरिङ्गितैर्गत्या चेष्टया भाषणेन च \| नेत्र-वक्त्र-विकारैश्च लक्ष्यतेऽन्तर्गतं मनः \|\| — The explicitly expressed meaning can be understood even by an animal, just as horses and elephants carry out commands when directed. The discerning person, however, infers what has not been said, for intelligence bears fruit in understanding another's indications. The mind within is discerned through appearance, gestures, gait, behavior, speech, and changes in the eyes and face. In this instance, யார்யார் is the person whose inner state may not be fully disclosed; எப்பொருள் includes both the words spoken and the accompanying indications conveyed through outward behavior; and மெய்ப்பொருள் காண்பது அறிவு is the ability to infer the unspoken thoughts and intentions underlying these expressions. Mahabharata Shanti Parva 12.308.90 (BORI): कामात्क्रोधाद्भयाल्लोभाद्दैन्यादानार्यकात्तथा \| ह्रीतोऽनुक्रोशतो मानान्न वक्ष्यामि कथंचन \|\| complements the Panchatantra by identifying the inner emotional and psychological conditions—desire, anger, fear, greed, shame, compassion, and pride—that leave their imprint on what a person says or chooses to withhold.

##### 4. Impartial extraction of beneficial truth

A fourth application of மெய்ப்பொருள் காண்பது அறிவு is the ability to separate useful or true substance from insults, criticism, hostile speech, unpleasant advice, or statements containing a mixture of truth and falsehood. Neither the speaker's relationship to the hearer nor the manner in which something is expressed should obscure its substance. This application is especially close to Parimelazhagar's observation that உறுதிப்பொருள் may be heard even from an enemy.

The Yoga Vasishtha makes this principle explicit in 2.18.69: विचारणादनुभवकारिवैरिणोऽपि वाङ्मयं त्वनुगतमस्मदादिषु \| स्त्रियोक्तमप्यपपरमार्थवैदिकं वचो वचःप्रलपनमेव नागमः \|\| — through careful inquiry, even the words of an enemy are to be accepted if they lead to genuine understanding. Likewise, words conveying the highest truth are not to be dismissed merely because of their source, whereas mere babble does not become authoritative teaching simply by being uttered. Here, யார்யார் encompasses even an adversary or a speaker whose standing might otherwise invite dismissal; எப்பொருள் is the substance of the utterance, particularly its capacity to yield genuine understanding; and மெய்ப்பொருள் காண்பது அறிவு is the inquiry that distinguishes such substance from empty or unreliable speech. The reference to विचारणा is particularly significant: the words are neither accepted nor rejected on the basis of their source, but tested through examination. This closely echoes Parimelazhagar's explanation that one must attend to the substance rather than the nature of the speaker.

Manu Smriti 2.239 offers a related maxim: विषादप्यमृतं ग्राह्यं बालादपि सुभाषितम् \| अमित्रादपि सद्वृत्तममेध्यादपि काञ्चनम् \|\| — one should take nectar even from poison, a good saying even from a child, good conduct even from an enemy, and gold even from an impure place. The opening metaphor, विषादप्यमृतं ग्राह्यम्, captures the broader principle of extracting what is valuable from an otherwise undesirable source. The subsequent examples reinforce this through speech and conduct alike, showing that neither the source nor its perceived worthiness should determine the value of what is received. Thus, the verse complements the Yoga Vasishtha's more direct treatment of an adversary's words.

A further dimension arises when the substance is beneficial but its expression is unpleasant. Vidura Niti, Mahabharata Udyoga Parva 5.37.14 (BORI), observes: सुलभाः पुरुषा राजन्सततं प्रियवादिनः \| अप्रियस्य तु पथ्यस्य वक्ता श्रोता च दुर्लभः \|\| — people who continually speak pleasant words are easily found, O King; but those who speak, and those who listen to, what is unpleasant yet beneficial are rare. The expression अप्रियस्य तु पथ्यस्य captures the distinction between the manner of delivery and the value of what is conveyed. Vidura's observation applies equally to the वक्ता and the श्रोता -- it is rare both to speak what is unpleasant yet beneficial and to listen to it with receptiveness. While the verse recognizes the difficulty on both sides, in my reading, Valluvar's focus here is on the latter—the hearer's ability to set aside the discomfort of an unwelcome message and discern its beneficial substance. 

Valmiki Ramayana provides a negative illustration in Yuddha Kanda 6.36.1: तत्तु माल्यवतो वाक्यं हितमुक्तं दशाननः \| न मर्षयति दुष्टात्मा कालस्य वशमागतः \|\| — Ravana, under the sway of time, cannot tolerate the beneficial words spoken by Malyavan. The explicit use of हितम् identifies the beneficial substance of Malyavan's counsel, while न मर्षयति expresses Ravana's inability to bear or tolerate it. His failure illustrates the converse of the kural's wisdom: rather than discerning the value of what is said, he allows his disposition and reaction to the advice to prevent him from receiving it.

##### 5. Verification of reports and allegations

A fifth application of மெய்ப்பொருள் காண்பது அறிவு is the verification of reports, accusations, and assertions concerning friends, relatives, associates, or rivals. Such information may come from a trusted person, a hostile adversary, or an unknown source; wisdom requires investigation rather than acceptance or rejection based on the reporter's identity or the emotional effect of the allegation. Kautilya's Arthashastra 3.1.46 provides a procedural illustration in the context of jurisprudence: दृष्टदोषः स्वयंवादः स्वपक्षपरपक्षयोः \| अनुयोगार्जवं हेतुः शपथश्चार्थसाधकः \|\| — a party's own assertion, whether on one's own side or the opposing side, may be found defective; examination, straightforwardness, evidence, and oath are among the means by which a claim is established. Here, யார்யார் encompasses the parties whose assertions are being examined; எப்பொருள் is the allegation or claim and the grounds advanced in its support; and மெய்ப்பொருள் காண்பது அறிவு is the process of testing those assertions through examination and evidence rather than accepting them at face value. Although set within judicial procedure, the verse illustrates a broader application of the kural's principle: discernment requires the verification of what is asserted, independently of the identity or affiliation of the person making the assertion.

#### The broader intellectual framework: Anvikshiki

Kautilya's Arthashastra 1.2.11 provides the broader intellectual framework within which these different applications can be understood: धर्माधर्मौ त्रय्यामर्थानर्थौ वार्त्तायां नयानयौ दण्डनीत्यां बलाबले चैव \| एतासां हेतुभिरन्वीक्षमाणा लोकस्योपकरोति व्यसनेऽभ्युदये च बुद्धिमवस्थापयति प्रज्ञावाक्यक्रियावैशारद्यं च करोति \|\|. Anviksiki, through reasoned examination, investigates right and wrong in the Vedas, gain and loss in economics, proper and improper policy in statecraft, and strength and weakness in the science of government. It benefits the world, steadies the intellect in adversity and prosperity, and produces excellence in wisdom, speech, and action. In the context of அரசியல் where this kural is situated, this provides a particularly apt framework for understanding மெய்ப்பொருள் காண்பது அறிவு as an essential faculty of governance: the ruler must subject information, counsel, allegations, and competing claims to reasoned examination, discerning what is true, sound, and beneficial before arriving at a judgment or taking action.


### Verse 4

I propose Bharavi's Kiratarjuniyam 14.2,4 as the primary parallel for this kural. The expression எண்பொருளவாகச் செலச்சொல்லி, the ability to convey subtle or complex meaning intelligibly, finds a parallel in गम्भीरमर्थं कतिचित् प्रकाशताम् (14.4), the rare ability to bring profound meaning into clarity. The complementary faculty, தான்பிறர்வாய் நுண்பொருள் காண்பது, is illustrated in 14.2 through सलेशम् उल्लिङ्गितशात्रवेङ्गितः, penetrating the adversary's strategic indications, and कृती गिरां विस्तरतत्त्वसंग्रहे, skill in gathering essential truths from extensive discourse. Bharavi thus portrays both the penetration of another's subtle meaning and the ability to make profound meaning intelligible. The correspondence is illustrative rather than exact: Bharavi describes Arjuna's particular intellectual and diplomatic abilities, whereas Valluvar formulates a general principle of communication and understanding.

### Deliberation over one's own speech and that of others

In the context of selecting and instructing envoys, Kamandakiya Nitisara 13.4 states: स भर्तुः शासनाद् गच्छेद् गन्तव्यं ह्य् उत्तरोत्तरम् \| स्ववाक्यपरवाक्यानाम् इति चेति च चिन्तयन् \|\|; he should proceed to the designated destination in accordance with his master's instructions, carefully considering his own words and the words of others, weighing the possibilities of what is said and what is meant. While this is not a direct parallel to the kural, its emphasis on deliberation over both one's own speech and another's speech is noteworthy: the intelligent envoy must consider the implications and intended meanings of what others say, while ensuring that his own words convey his intended meaning clearly, corresponding to both தான்பிறர்வாய் நுண்பொருள் காண்பது and எண்பொருளவாகச் செலச்சொல்லி.

Mahabharata Udyoga Parva 5.33.28 (BORI) makes the communicative dimension of intelligence more explicit: प्रवृत्तवाक् चित्रकथ ऊहवान् प्रतिभानवान् \| आशु ग्रन्थस्य वक्ता च स वै पण्डित उच्यते \|\|. The wise person is characterized by fluency of speech, the ability to present ideas engagingly, capacity for reasoning, intellectual presence of mind, and the ability to explain authoritative texts readily. The verse thus treats communicative competence not as an incidental accomplishment but as an integral attribute of the learned person. In particular, ऊहवान् and आशु ग्रन्थस्य वक्ता resonate with the combination of intellectual discernment and lucid expression in Valluvar's formulation.

Another conceptual correspondence occurs in Mahbharata Shanti Parva 12.308.96, in the dialogue between Sulabha and Janaka: यस्तु वक्ता द्वयोरर्थमविरुद्धं प्रभाषते \| श्रोतुश्चैवात्मनश्चैव स वक्ता नेतरो नृप \|\|. The true speaker, O King, is one who speaks in a manner consistent with the meaning intended by himself and intelligible to the listener; no other deserves that designation. The surrounding discussion concerns the conditions of meaningful and proper communication, including the relationship between words, intended meaning, and the understanding of the listener. In the larger exchange, Sulabha challenges Janaka's assumptions through carefully reasoned speech, exposing the limitations of relying on verbal expressions without understanding their actual import. The verse is particularly relevant to எண்பொருளவாகச் செலச்சொல்லி: successful communication requires that the intended meaning reach the listener without distortion. It also complements தான்பிறர்வாய் நுண்பொருள் காண்பது by emphasizing that the speaker must understand the relationship between what is expressed and what is understood.

#### प्रसाद: clarity of expression in poetics and the performing arts

The Sanskrit literary tradition develops the ideal of lucid expression through the concept of प्रसाद, one of the recognized qualities of poetic composition. Although these discussions concern literary and aesthetic expression rather than the entirety of Valluvar's teaching, they provide a particularly illuminating explanation of எண்பொருளவாகச் செலச்சொல்லி.

Bharata's Natyashastra 16.100 defines प्रसाद as follows: अप्यनुक्तो बुधैर्यत्र शब्दोऽर्थो वा प्रतीयते \| सुखशब्दार्थसंयोगात् प्रसादः परिकीर्तितः \|\|. Even something not explicitly stated, whether a word or a meaning, is understood by the discerning because of the natural and effortless connection between expression and meaning; this is called प्रसाद. The expression सुखशब्दार्थसंयोगात् प्रसादः is especially relevant. It identifies lucidity not merely with the simplicity of individual words but with the felicitous relationship between words and meaning, through which understanding arises without difficulty. This offers an aesthetic articulation of Valluvar's expectation that even subtle or complex meaning should be conveyed intelligibly.

Bhamaha develops the same principle in Kavyalankara 2.1 and 2.3. He states in 2.1: माधुर्यमभिवाञ्छन्तः प्रसादं च सुमेधसः \| समासवन्ति भूयांसि न पदानि प्रयुञ्जते \|\|. Those of refined understanding who seek sweetness and lucidity do not employ an excessive number of compound expressions. He then makes the criterion of accessibility particularly vivid in 2.3: श्रव्यं नातिसमस्तार्थं काव्यं मधुरमिष्यते \| आविद्वदङ्गनाबालप्रतीतार्थं प्रसादवत् \|\|. Poetry that is pleasing to hear and not excessively burdened with compounds is considered sweet; it possesses प्रसाद when its meaning is readily understood by everyone, from the learned to women and children. The progression from माधुर्यम् and प्रसादम् in 2.1 to आविद्वदङ्गनाबालप्रतीतार्थम् in 2.3 makes the point especially clear: lucidity is measured by the accessibility of meaning, not by the complexity of expression. These are not direct parallels to the entire kural, but they illuminate the literary and aesthetic dimension of எண்பொருளவாகச் செலச்சொல்லி.

#### A contemporary resonance: Postel's Robustness Principle

Although far removed from Valluvar's immediate context, the kural also has an interesting resonance in modern communications technology. Jon Postel's Robustness Principle, commonly expressed as "Be conservative in what you send, be liberal in what you accept," is an important maxim in the design of communication protocols. Among other things, it encourages systems to generate outputs that are easy for the receiving system to interpret while being capable of accepting inputs that may be complex or imperfectly formed. It illustrates the continuing relevance of the kural's two-sided conception of communication: clarity and intelligibility in what one conveys, coupled with the capacity to interpret complexity in what one receives. 


### Verse 5

I propose Yoga Vasishtha 4.61.2,6 as the primary parallel for this Kural. The expression नापदा म्लानिमायान्ति, illustrated by the golden lotus that does not wither at night (निशि हेमाम्बुजं यथा), closely recalls Valluvar's image of wisdom that neither blossoms nor closes (மலர்தலுங் கூம்பலுமில்லது). The constancy of this disposition is further conveyed by ते नित्यमेव मुदिताः प्रकाशाः, describing those endowed with great qualities as invariably joyful and radiant. The expression राजससात्त्विक्या जाता भुवि महागुणाः also offers a suggestive correspondence to உலகந் தழீஇய தொட்பு, describing those born into the world with a nature combining energy and clarity. Wisdom (அறிவு) is implicit in the Yoga Vasishtha's broader concern with discriminative understanding, although the correspondence here is primarily through the imagery of unchanging disposition rather than an explicit definition of wisdom.

This reading is strengthened by Parimelazhagar's interpretation of உலகந் தழீஇய தொட்பு as making the worthy one's friends (உலகத்தை நட்பாக்குவது ஒருவனுக்கு ஒட்பமாம்). He understands அறிவு as remaining of one disposition in that friendship, without first blossoming and subsequently closing (அந்நட்பின்கண் முன் மலர்தலும் பின் கூம்புதலும் இன்றி ஒரு நிலையனாவது அறிவாம்). His gloss brings out an important relational dimension: the constancy described by Valluvar is not merely an inward equanimity but also an evenness of disposition in one's dealings with others.

Bhagavad Gita 2.57 provides a particularly close corroboration through its definition of the person of established wisdom: यः सर्वत्रानभिस्नेहस्तत्तत्प्राप्य शुभाशुभम् \| नाभिनन्दति न द्वेष्टि तस्य प्रज्ञा प्रतिष्ठिता \|\|. The expression नाभिनन्दति न द्वेष्टि (neither rejoices nor recoils) offers a conceptual parallel to மலர்தலும் கூம்பலும் இல்லது: the wise person does not alternately blossom and wither in response to changing circumstances. The paired expression शुभाशुभम् encompasses both favorable and unfavorable experiences, in the face of which this equanimity is maintained. Finally, तस्य प्रज्ञा प्रतिष्ठिता (his wisdom is firmly established) directly corroborates Valluvar's identification of such constancy with அறிவு. The Gita thus makes explicit the connection between freedom from reactive attachment and established wisdom that is implicit in the Kural.

Kautilya's Arthashastra 6.1.6 extends this principle into the domain of statecraft by including steadiness of disposition among the personal excellences of a ruler: संवृतोऽदीनाभिहास्यजिह्मभ्रुकुटीक्षणः \| कामक्रोधलोभस्तम्भचापलोपतापपैशुन्यहीनः \| श्लक्ष्णः स्मितोदग्राभिभाषी वृद्धोपदेशाचारः इत्यात्मसम्पत् \|\| The relevant qualities include संवृतः (restrained and composed), अदीनः (not downcast), freedom from चापल (fickleness), and स्मितोदग्राभिभाषी (speaking graciously with a smile). These are not prescriptions for maintaining a composed public face while inwardly remaining agitated. By enumerating them as constituents of आत्मसम्पत्, Kautilya presents them as qualities arising from the ruler's own cultivated disposition. His outward constancy is therefore the expression of inward restraint and dispassion, rather than a performance adopted for the benefit of others.

The Yoga Vasishtha and Bhagavad Gita thus converge on the inward principle: one established in wisdom remains undisturbed by changing circumstances, neither flourishing nor withering in response to them. Kautilya's Arthashastra then gives this principle its practical manifestation in statecraft, showing how steadiness of disposition becomes an essential component of a ruler's personal excellence.


### Verse 6

I propose Yoga Vasishtha 3.118.19 as the primary parallel for this kural. The expression सर्वाचारक्रमागतम् (conduct transmitted through the succession of established customs) corresponds to எவ்வ துறைவ துலகம், while आचारमाचरन्त्येव सुप्रबुद्धवदक्षतम् (they conduct themselves accordingly, just as one fully awakened would) corresponds to உலகத்தோடு அவ்வ துறைவ தறிவு. The reference to पार्श्वस्थबोधिताः (prompted by those around them) further reinforces the social dimension of the correspondence. सुप्रबुद्ध conveys the sense of being fully awake or awakened, illuminating the discernment implied by அறிவு. The correspondence is particularly close because the verse brings together both the established conduct of the world and the wisdom of conducting oneself in accordance with it.

The Taittiriya Upanishad 1.11.3–4 provides a corresponding injunction in Shruti, addressed to students completing their Vedic education: अथ यदि ते कर्मविचिकित्सा वा वृत्तविचिकित्सा वा स्यात् \| ये तत्र ब्राह्मणाः संमर्शिनः \| युक्ता आयुक्ताः \| अलूक्षा धर्मकामाः स्युः \| यथा ते तत्र वर्तेरन् \| तथा तत्र वर्तेथाः \|\|. The passage instructs the student that, should any doubt arise concerning an action or a matter of conduct, he should conduct himself as do the reflective, well-grounded, gentle, and dharma-oriented Brahmanas in that place. எவ்வ துறைவ துலகம் corresponds to यथा ते तत्र वर्तेरन्, the conduct of those in one's surroundings, while உலகத்தோடு அவ்வ துறைவ தறிவு corresponds to तथा तत्र वर्तेथाः, the discernment to shape one's own conduct accordingly. The Upanishad thus qualifies this emulation by directing the student to follow exemplary, dharma-oriented persons rather than conform indiscriminately to prevailing practice.

Manu Smriti 2.18 further corroborates the customary dimension of the principle: तस्मिन् देशे य आचारः पारम्पर्यक्रमागतः \| वर्णानां सान्तरालानां स सदाचार उच्यते \|\|. Manu identifies as सदाचार the conduct traditionally handed down in a particular country among its constituent social groups. The expression तस्मिन् देशे य आचारः corresponds to எவ்வ துறைவ துலகம், identifying the customary conduct prevailing in a particular country, while स सदाचार उच्यते establishes the discernment to conduct oneself in accordance with that accepted practice, as expressed in உலகத்தோடு அவ்வ துறைவ தறிவு.

A different, specifically relational and potentially political reading emerges from Vidura Niti, Mahabharata, Udyoga Parva 5.37.6 (BORI): यस्मिन्यथा वर्तते यो मनुष्यस्तस्मिंस्तथा वर्तितव्यं स धर्मः \| मायाचारो मायया वर्तितव्यः साध्वाचारः साधुना प्रत्युपेयः \|\|. One should behave towards a person in the manner in which that person behaves towards oneself: that is dharma. Deceit should be met with deceit, and good conduct should be reciprocated with good conduct. Here the correspondence is directly reciprocal: यस्मिन् यथा वर्तते यो मनुष्यः corresponds to எவ்வ துறைவ துலகம், while तस्मिंस्तथा वर्तितव्यम् corresponds to உலகத்தோடு அவ்வ துறைவ தறிவு. Unlike the preceding parallels, which concern conformity to established social or exemplary conduct, Vidura articulates a principle of reciprocity, extending even to the strategic response to deceit. This anticipates the modern tit-for-tat strategy in game theory, developed by Anatol Rapoport and popularized by Robert Axelrod's iterated Prisoner's Dilemma tournaments: one begins cooperatively and thereafter reciprocates the other party's previous action. Given the kural's placement in the அரசியல் portion of பொருட்பால், this reading is worth considering as a statecraft application—the discernment to deal with others in accordance with their own conduct. Vidura's formulation thus offers a more specifically relational and political interpretation of the broader principle expressed in the kural.


### Verse 7

I propose Bhartrihari's Nitishatakam 100 as the primary parallel for this kural, read in conjunction with Manakkudavar's gloss. Bhartrihari states that the wise person must carefully ascertain the eventual outcome of an undertaking, whether beneficial or harmful (कार्यजातं गुणवदगुणवद्वा कुर्वता परिणतिरवधार्या यत्नतः पण्डितेन). This corresponds closely to அறிவுடையார் ஆவது அறிவார், with परिणति capturing the eventual outcome and ramifications of an undertaking. The second half of Bhartrihari's verse illustrates the consequences of lacking such discernment: actions performed in excessive haste produce a painful outcome, like a thorn lodged in the heart (अतिरभसकृतानां कर्मणामाविपत्तेर् भवति हृदयदाही शल्यतुल्यो विपाकः). This is a metaphorical illustration rather than a direct formulation of the unwise person's inability to foresee. Manakkudavar's gloss reinforces this reading: பிற்பயக்குமது அறிவார் அறிவுடையாராவார்; அதனை யறியாதவர் அறிவில்லாதவராவர் — the wise are those who know what an undertaking will yield subsequently, while those who do not know this are unwise.

An alternative interpretation is possible in light of Kamandakiya Nitisara XII.11: शक्याशक्यपरिच्छेदं कुर्याद् बुद्ध्या प्रसन्नया \| केवलं दन्तभङ्गाय दन्तिनः शैलताडनम् \|\|. One should employ clear intelligence to distinguish what is feasible from what is infeasible; an elephant that strikes a mountain merely breaks its tusks. Here, शक्याशक्यपरिच्छेदम् offers a distinct reading of ஆவது அறிவார்: the wise understand what can actually be accomplished, whereas the unwise undertake futile or impossible tasks and suffer the consequences. This is not simply another formulation of foreseeing an undertaking's eventual outcome, but a complementary application of practical intelligence in assessing feasibility before undertaking it.

The connection between foresight and practical intelligence is particularly pertinent to the selection of ministers. Kamandakiya Nitisara IV.32 states: साधु तेषाम् अमात्यानां तद्विद्येभ्यश् च बुद्धिमान् \| चक्षुष्मत्तां च शिल्पं च परीक्षेत गुणद्वयम् \|\| The prescription to examine a prospective minister for चक्षुष्मत्ता, understood in the technical Arthashastra sense as foresight (Apte, s.v. चक्षुष्मत्, sense 3, citing Kautilya, Arthashastra 1.9), is especially relevant to ஆவது அறிவார். It establishes the ability to anticipate developments and their consequences as a specific qualification of those entrusted with state responsibilities, thus giving Valluvar's conception of foresight (ஆவது அறிவார்) a direct application in ministerial selection.

Valmiki Ramayana 2.63.7 offers a close corroboration of the primary reading: गुरुलाघवमर्थानामारम्भे कर्मणां फलम् \| दोषं वा यो न जानाति स बाल इति होच्यते \|\|. One who, at the commencement of an undertaking, does not understand the relative weight of its consequences, whether beneficial or harmful, is called a child, that is, an immature person. The passage occurs as Dasharatha, grieving over Rama's exile, reflects on the consequences of his own earlier actions. The correspondence is particularly evident in ஆவது அறிவார் and कर्मणां फलम् दोषं वा यो न जानाति -- wisdom consists in discerning the fruits and possible harm of an action before embarking upon it. The expression गुरुलाघवम् further introduces the consideration of the relative weight or significance of those consequences, while स बाल इति होच्यते supplies the converse in the kural: one who lacks this discernment is அறிவிலார்.

Mahabharata, Shanti Parva 12.138.35 (BORI Critical Edition) introduces a further dimension of foresight in the context of statecraft: अनागतं विजानीयाद्यच्छेद्भयमुपस्थितम् \| पुनर्वृद्धिक्षयात्किञ्चिदभिवृत्तं निशामयेत् \|\|. One should discern what is yet to come, avert the danger that has arisen, and observe developments through their increase and decline. This extends ஆவது அறிவார் to the discernment of impending developments and the assessment of circumstances as they evolve. Unlike Bhartrihari's broader concern with the eventual outcome of an undertaking, the Mahabharata is more specifically concerned with anticipating dangers, responding to present threats, and observing changing conditions.

Yoga Vasistha 5.12.19 offers another perspective on this faculty of discernment: यस्योज्ज्वलति तीक्ष्णाग्रा पूर्वापरविचारिणी \| प्रज्ञादीपशिखा जातु जाड्यान्ध्यं तं न बाधते \|\| The flame of prajna, whose sharp point examines what precedes and what follows, prevents the darkness of intellectual dullness from overcoming the discerning person. The expression पूर्वापरविचारिणी is particularly relevant to ஆவது அறிவார்: intelligence examines both what has transpired and what is yet to come, discerning future outcomes in the light of preceding events. Conversely, one in whom this faculty is absent falls within அறிவிலார் — those who lack the discernment to anticipate what is to come. This complements the primary parallel by emphasizing the temporal and analytical dimension of foresight.


### Verse 8

I propose Mahabharata, Shanti Parva 12.138.200–201 as the primary parallel for this kural. The first verse prescribes maintaining oneself as though apprehensive (भीतवत् संनिधिः कार्यः), and identifies the resulting vigilance and sustained effort as the source of intelligence (भयादुत्पद्यते बुद्धिः). The second makes Valluvar's distinction particularly explicit: न भयं विद्यते राजन् भीतस्यानागते भये — one who anticipates danger before it arrives has no reason to fear it; conversely, from the overconfidence of one who remains unafraid arises great danger (अभीतस्य च विश्रम्भात् सुमहज्जायते भयम्). Thus அஞ்சுவ தஞ்சாமை பேதைமை is illustrated by the grave consequences of failing to fear what warrants fear, while அஞ்சுவ தஞ்ச லறிவார் தொழில் finds its counterpart in the deliberate vigilance that anticipates danger and generates बुद्धिः. The Mahabharata also relates to an aspect of the earlier kural in this chapter, அறிவுடையா ராவ தறிவார், through its emphasis on recognizing impending danger before it materializes.

Kamandakiya Nitisara XII.9 provides a close corroboration of the positive prescription in the kural: पश्यद्भिर् दूरतोऽपायान् सूपायप्रतिपत्तिभिः \| भवन्तीह फलायैव विद्वद्भिर् मन्त्रिताः क्रियाः \|\|. Actions deliberated by the wise, who perceive dangers from afar and adopt appropriate means, become fruitful. The verse brings together three elements particularly relevant to அஞ்சுவ தஞ்ச லறிவார் தொழில்: अपायान्, the dangers and sources of harm to be anticipated; दूरतो पश्यद्भिः, the ability to perceive them from afar; and विद्वद्भिर् मन्त्रिताः क्रियाः, actions deliberated upon by the wise. The emphasis is not merely on recognizing danger but on anticipating it and taking appropriate measures. Kamandakiya XII.13 offers a metaphorical corroboration of the converse: नाग्निं पतङ्गवद् गच्छेत् स्पृश्यम् एव तु संस्पृशेत् \| किम् अन्यत् स्याद् ऋते दाहात् पतङ्गस्याग्निम् ऋच्छतः \|\|. One should not approach fire like a moth, but touch only what is safe to touch; what else can happen to the moth that approaches fire except to be burnt? The moth's inability to recognize and avoid an obvious danger illustrates அஞ்சுவ தஞ்சாமை பேதைமை. Together, these two Kamandakiya verses capture both the anticipatory intelligence and the folly of disregarding danger.

Valmiki Ramayana, Aranyakanda 3.31.16 offers a further corroboration of the negative prescription: नानुतिष्ठति कार्याणि भयेषु न बिभेति च \| क्षिप्रं राज्याच्च्युतो दीनस्तृणैस्तुल्यो भविष्यति \|\|. He who fails to attend to his duties and does not fear when dangers arise will soon be deprived of his kingdom, become wretched, and be like a blade of grass. te. The expression भयेषु न बिभेति corresponds directly to அஞ்சுவ தஞ்சாமை, while the resulting loss of sovereignty illustrates why such conduct is பேதைமை. The positive prescription, அஞ்சுவ தஞ்ச லறிவார் தொழில், remains implicit: the ruler's responsibility includes the discernment to fear what must be feared.

Hitopadesha 1.57 introduces an important qualification concerning the timing and proper exercise of fear: तावद्भयस्य भेतव्यं यावद्भयम् अनागतम् \| आगतं तु भयं वीक्ष्य नरः कुर्याद् यथोचितम् \|\|. One should fear danger so long as it has not yet arrived; but when danger actually materializes, one should act appropriately upon observing it. This distinguishes anticipatory caution from the action required once danger is present. The wisdom lies not in perpetual apprehension, but in recognizing the appropriate response at each stage: anticipating danger before its arrival and acting decisively when it materializes. This provides a useful qualification to அஞ்சுவ தஞ்ச லறிவார் தொழில்.

#### A Spiritual Interpretation of What is to be Feared

Yoga Vasistha 2.18.47 offers a secondary ethical reading of the kural: एतावत्यपि येऽभीताः पापा भोगरसे स्थिताःस्वमातृविष्ठाकृमयः कीर्तनीया न तेऽधमाः \|\|. The passage condemns those who, despite instruction, remain unafraid and continue to be immersed in the pleasures of worldly indulgence, comparing them to worms living in their own mother's excrement. Here येऽभीताः पापा and तेऽधमाः can be read in relation to அஞ்சுவ தஞ்சாமை பேதைமை, with the object of appropriate fear being sin and its consequences. The context is important: Yoga Vasistha 2.18.40 employs the metaphor of a painted snake to distinguish the unreal from the real: चित्रसर्पः परिज्ञातो न सर्पभयदो यथा सर्पभयदो दृश्यसर्पः परिज्ञातस्तथा न सुखदुःखदः \|\|. Just as a painted snake, once recognized as a painting, does not cause the fear that a real snake does, so the visible world, when understood in its true nature, no longer produces pleasure or pain. The distinction between what is genuinely to be feared and what is not thus introduces a further dimension of discriminative wisdom. This is a secondary, inward and ethical application rather than a direct statecraft parallel.

#### Arthashastra's perspective on அஞ்சுவ தஞ்ச லறிவார் தொழில் 

Kautilya's Arthashastra 9.7 provides a particularly substantial account of the practical intelligence underlying அஞ்சுவ தஞ்ச லறிவார் தொழில். It begins by identifying the arising of fear from oneself or from another as an adverse condition: स्वतः परतो वा भय-उत्पत्तिरित्यनर्थः ॥ (9.7.7). The following verse requires the assessment of whether a proposed course of action is advantageous or disadvantageous: तयोः अर्थो न वा इति \| अनर्थो न वा इति \| अर्थोऽनर्थ इति \| अनर्थोऽर्थ इति संशयः \|\| (9.7.8). Kautilya's concern is therefore not simply the recognition of danger, but the reasoned assessment of the actual consequences of a proposed action, including the possibility that an apparent advantage may conceal a disadvantage, or vice versa.

The subsequent discussion develops this method systematically. Arthashastra 9.7.5–6 identifies gains that are actually perilous because they strengthen an enemy, create future liabilities, or entail depletion and expenditure; 9.7.9–13 examines concrete situations in which the strategic value of an action is uncertain. Verse 9.7.14 introduces a sixfold classification of consequences, distinguishing immediate gains and losses from their further consequences. The discussion in 9.7.23–45 then examines combinations of gains, losses, and uncertainties arising from different directions, prescribing appropriate responses, including seeking allies and withdrawing from untenable positions. This analytical framework gives considerable practical content to Valluvar's prescription: அஞ்சுவது அஞ்சல் is not mere apprehension, but the exercise of discriminative intelligence in identifying, assessing, and managing risks. The treatment also connects with அறிவுடையா ராவ தறிவார், since the assessment of an apparent gain or loss must account for its eventual consequences.


### Verse 9

I propose Kamandakiya Nitisara XII.20 and XII.24 as the primary parallel for this kural. Kamandaka identifies व्याधि (disease), alongside fire, water, famine, and pestilence, among the five natural calamities. He then assigns their prevention or remediation, together with the protection of the king and kingdom, to the minister. The lexical correspondence is particularly rich between எதிரதாக் காக்கும் and व्यसनस्य प्रतीकारो राजराज्याभिरक्षणम्, which means counteracting calamities and protecting the king and kingdom. Manakkudavar glosses எதிரதாக் காக்கும் as துன்பம் வருவதற்கு முன்பே வருமென்று நினைத்துக் காக்கும், while the dictionary defines எதிரதாக் காத்தல் as வரக்கடவதாகிய ஒன்றை முன்னே அறிந்து அதன் காரணத்தை விலக்குதல். The primary sense of எதிரதாக் is thus anticipation of what is yet to come, but எதிர் also carries the connotations of adversity and action directed against something. Correspondingly, व्यसन denotes calamity or adversity, while प्रतीकारः denotes counteraction, obviation, prevention, or remedy; the prefix प्रति further reinforces the sense of action directed against. The semantic range of காக்கும் is equally well accommodated: it encompasses protection (பாதுகாத்தல்), governance (அரசாளுதல்), anticipation (எதிர்பார்த்தல்), and removal or avoidance (விலக்குதல்). These converge in राजराज्याभिरक्षणम्, which signifies the comprehensive protection of king and kingdom. Thus the Sanskrit expression does not merely reproduce the idea of guarding against impending calamity; its constituent terms collectively accommodate the several interrelated senses embedded in Valluvar's compact expression.

Manakkudavar glosses அதிர வருவதோர் நோய் as நடுங்க வருவதொரு துன்பம். This corresponds to व्यसनम्, denoting calamity or adversity; the enumeration of the five प्रकृतिव्यसनानि in XII.20, with व्याधि providing the direct equivalent of நோய், makes the correspondence particularly precise. இல்லை finds its counterpart in हन्ति तद्व्यसनान्वितः, which states that the minister who undertakes this responsibility destroys the calamity, leaving no adversity to afflict the king or kingdom.

அறிவினார் is implicit in the ministerial responsibility expressed by अमात्यस्य कर्मेदम्. The qualification of the minister who can discharge this responsibility is illuminated by Kamandakiya IV.32: साधु तेषाम् अमात्यानां तद्विद्येभ्यश् च बुद्धिमान् \| चक्षुष्मत्तां च शिल्पं च परीक्षेत गुणद्वयम् \|\|. The prescription to examine an amatya for चक्षुष्मत्ता, understood in the technical Arthashastra sense as foresight (Apte, s.v. चक्षुष्मत्, sense 3, citing Kautilya, Arthashastra 1.9), helps explain the force of எதிரதாக் காக்கும் அறிவினார்: intelligence here is the capacity to perceive impending adversity and take measures against it in advance. The correspondence therefore brings out Valluvar's conception of intelligence not merely as the ability to respond to adversity, but as the foresight to prevent it.

Agni Purana 214.14–17, in its chapter on political science, reproduces verses almost identical to Kamandakiya Nitisara XII.20 and XII.24, with only minor textual and metrical variations. It thus provides an equivalent Puranic parallel to the same political principle.

At a more general philosophical level, Patanjali's Yogasutra 2.16 states हेयं दुःखमनागतम् — suffering that has not yet arisen is to be avoided. This expresses the underlying principle of preventing future suffering, but Valluvar gives it a specifically political and administrative application: the wise person anticipates adversity and guards against it through timely action. Kamandakiya Nitisara goes considerably further, elaborating in the surrounding chapters the various calamities that must be anticipated and the measures prescribed for their prevention or remediation. This detailed treatment illuminates the scope of Valluvar's otherwise cryptic reference to impending adversity.


### Verse 10

I propose Brihat Chanakya Niti 10.1 as the primary parallel for this kural. The second line, विद्यारत्नेन हीनो यः स हीनः सर्ववस्तुषु, states that one who lacks the jewel of vidya is deficient in every respect, closely corresponding to அறிவிலா ரென்னுடைய ரேனு மிலர். The positive half, அறிவுடையா ரெல்லா முடையார், is implicit in the contrast: the possession of vidya constitutes true wealth, irrespective of material possessions. Vidya here need not be restricted to formal education or technical learning; it can be understood more broadly as discriminative knowledge and wisdom. Kalingar's gloss provides a useful bridge between vidya and அறிவு: கற்றும் கேட்டும் கசடு அற்ற அறிவுடையோரை மற்று எல்லாம் உடையரென்று உணரப்படும்; ஆதலால் மற்று அங்ஙனம் தெளிந்த அறிவிலாதார் வேறு யாதானும் உடையரேனும் யாதும் இலர் என்றவாறு. Learning and listening, when they produce கசடற்ற அறிவு, constitute true possession; those lacking such clear discernment possess nothing of real worth, even if they own other things.

Hitopadesha 0.6 further illuminates the breadth of the possessions associated with vidya: विद्या ददाति विनयं विनयाद् याति पात्रताम् \| पात्रत्वाद् धनमाप्नोति धनाद्धर्मं ततः सुखम् \|\|. Vidya gives humility; from humility comes worthiness; worthiness brings wealth; wealth enables dharma, and from dharma comes happiness. This progression helps explain the breadth of எல்லா முடையார்: the fruits of wisdom extend beyond material prosperity to humility, character and worthiness, dharma, and ultimately sukha. Vidya is therefore not merely an acquisition in itself but the foundation for a succession of personal, material, and ethical benefits.

Yoga Vasistha 5.12.26 and 5.12.28 develop the positive proposition in the kural by presenting wisdom as the source of all other acquisitions. The former states: य एव यत्नः क्रियते बाह्यार्थोपार्जने जनैः \| स एव यत्नः कर्तव्यः पूर्वं प्रज्ञाविवर्धने \|\|. The very effort that people expend in acquiring external wealth should first be directed toward cultivating wisdom. The latter extends this proposition further: स्वर्गाद्यद्यच्च पातालाद्राज्याद्यत्समवाप्यते \| तत्समासाद्यते सर्वं प्रज्ञाकोशान्महात्मना \|\|. Whatever can be obtained from heaven, the netherworld, or a kingdom is attained by the great person from the treasury of wisdom. The expression तत्समासाद्यते सर्वं प्रज्ञाकोशान्महात्मना corresponds particularly well to அறிவுடையா ரெல்லா முடையார்: all possessions are attainable through the treasury of wisdom. The injunction to direct one's efforts first toward प्रज्ञाविवर्धने, the cultivation of wisdom, also reinforces the converse: external acquisitions alone cannot compensate for the absence of அறிவு.

#### Contentment as another dimension of wisdom

An alternative interpretation of அறிவிலா ரென்னுடைய ரேனு மிலர் is that those who lack wisdom also lack contentment (सन्तोष). Whatever they may possess, they are never satisfied and continue to perceive themselves as wanting. In contrast, the wise, being content, have everything, for they want for nothing: அறிவுடையா ரெல்லா முடையார். This may be seen as resonating with Cicero's Omnia mea mecum porto (“All that is mine I carry with me”), understood here to suggest that one's true possessions are the enduring riches of wisdom and character rather than material things.

Hitopadesha 1.136 offers a related perspective: सर्वाः सम्पत्तयस्तस्य सन्तुष्टं यस्य मानसम् \| उपानद्गूढपादस्य ननु चर्मावृतेव भूः \|\|. All forms of prosperity belong to the person whose mind is content, just as one whose feet are protected by shoes has, as it were, the entire earth covered in leather. The Siddha-Siddhanta-Paddhati, attributed to Gorakshanatha, provides a conceptual bridge by enumerating contentment among the qualities of cultivated intelligence: विवेको वैराग्यं शान्तिः सन्तोषः क्षमा इति पञ्चगुणा बुद्धिः \|\|. Discrimination, dispassion, tranquillity, contentment, and forbearance are the five qualities of buddhi. Thus, the two verses illuminate the relationship between அறிவு and contentment: the latter is both a quality of cultivated intelligence and the basis of a sense of fullness, without which possessions never satisfy.
